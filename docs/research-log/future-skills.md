## R1. Day of AI, K-2 lessons
- **Finding (M):** I found no official US K-2 lesson list. The Australian Day of AI "Years 1-2" stream has these lessons: "What is Artificial Intelligence?", "The 3 Steps of AI", "How AI Senses Things", "Designing an AI Robot" and "Using AI for Creativity" (parts 1-2). Lessons run 30-60 min. The program starts everyone on "What is AI?" and then lets the teacher choose further units (a spiral design).
- A Day of AI 2022 K-2 machine-learning lesson (rehosted on aiforca) has children **look at a labeled dataset and find problems in the data that could make a computer predict wrongly**. A 3-5 deck uses a cat/dog classifier.
- **Not verified:** a "Pickles the dog" lesson. No result found it, so the spec does not cite it.
- **Implication:** the 3-step model (input → processing/learning → output) becomes the backbone of `AI.WHAT`. "Find the bad data" goes into `AI.DATA.1` at gan level. "Design an AI robot" becomes the bridge from `AI.WHAT` to `AI.CREATE`.
- Sources: [UQ Schools Net, DoAI delivery 2025](https://uqschoolsnet.com.au/files/6266/DoAI%20-%20How%20to%20Deliver%20the%20Program%202025.pdf); [Day of AI program](https://dayofai.org/?p=20); [aiforca K-2 ML](https://sites.google.com/view/aiforca/you-can-teach-ai/k-2/machine-learning-k-2); [Day of AI: How do machines learn](https://dayofai.org/units/how-do-machines-learn)

## R3. AI curricula for under-7s with outcome data (PopBots)
- **Finding (H for existence, M for numbers):** PopBots (Williams, Park & Breazeal, AAAI/CHI 2019) taught knowledge-based systems, supervised ML and generative AI to 80 pre-K and K children aged 4-6/7, using a social robot. The median cumulative assessment score was 70%. **Knowledge-based (rule) systems were understood best.** Children came to see robots as "learning" machines. Younger children saw robots as toys smarter than themselves, older children as people less smart than themselves.
- **Implication:** rule-based "if → then" robots are the right *first* AI concept for gan (`AI.WHAT.2`, `AI.PROMPT.2`). Supervised learning comes second. The anthropomorphism risk is real, which supports the visible-gears machine persona.
- Sources: [AAAI PopBots](https://ojs.aaai.org/index.php/AAAI/article/view/5040); [MIT DSpace "A is for AI"](https://dspace.mit.edu/handle/1721.1/137141); [Media Lab](https://www.media.mit.edu/publications/a-is-for-ai/)

## R4. Teachable-Machine-style activities for kids
- **Finding (M):** the standard classroom designs are (a) rock-paper-scissors image classification as an intro to bias (Australian Digital Technologies Hub), and (b) cats vs. dogs trained on a **deliberately uneven dataset** (more and more varied cats) so the model favors cats, followed by a discussion of bias (Canada Learning Code). The guidance says to **avoid having students photograph themselves or others** and to offer pre-collected images. Teachable Machine has an iPad-capable flow, but no source designs it for under-8s. A robot "puppy" trained with 1-D KNN on "pat vs. stroke" (Tufts CEEO) is a tactile analogue.
- **Implication:** `AI.DATA.2` (K1) uses **drawings or objects, never faces**. The classifier is a tiny on-device nearest-neighbour over Skia stroke features (no camera needed in v1). The bias lesson is built in with an uneven default dataset.
- Sources: [Digital Technologies Hub: AI image recognition](https://www.digitaltechnologieshub.edu.au/teach-and-assess/classroom-resources/lesson-ideas/ai-image-recognition-exploring-limitations-and-bias/); [RPS lesson sequence](https://www.digitaltechnologieshub.edu.au/media/3h5d43i0/rock-paper-scissors_lesson-sequence_acv9.docx); [Canada Learning Code: Cats, Dogs & ML](https://www.canadalearningcode.ca/wp-content/uploads/cats-dogs-ml-download.pdf); [CEEO Spike AI puppy](https://ceeoinnovations.github.io/RoboticsPlayground/playlists/SpikeAIPuppy.html); [ecampusontario: Teachable Machine & bias](https://ecampusontario.pressbooks.pub/artificialintelligenceineducationconference/chapter/teachable-machine-critical-ai-literacy-teaching-ai-bias/)

## R6. Project Zero thinking routines
- **Finding (H):** See-Think-Wonder uses the questions "What do you see? / What do you think about that? / What does it make you wonder?". Students often need a follow-up prompt per stage, such as "What else…?". Think-Puzzle-Explore asks what you already know, what puzzles you, and how you could explore it, and works best at the start of a unit to surface misconceptions. Project Zero has an early-childhood toolbox of 100+ routines. PZ handouts **may not be used commercially**. That does not matter for a private family app (A1), but **we still write our own Hebrew wording and do not copy handouts**. Evidence grade C, unchanged from the earlier note.
- Sources: [PZ See Think Wonder PDF](https://pz.harvard.edu/sites/default/files/See%20Think%20Wonder_0.pdf); [PZ Thinking Routine Matrix](https://pz.harvard.edu/sites/default/files/Thinking%20Routine%20Matrix_3.pdf); [NMWA HPZ intro](https://nmwa.org/wp-content/uploads/2022/03/HPZ-Thinking-Routine-intro-and-supporting-docs.pdf); [Tec observatory, early childhood](https://observatory.tec.mx/?p=19056)

## R7. Habits of Mind (Costa & Kallick)
- **Finding (M; the full list was not shown in any single extract):** there are 16 habits, and the authors say the list is not exhaustive. ## R8. Pólya heuristics for young children
- **Finding (M):** Pólya's 4 steps are understand, plan, carry out, look back. Elementary strategy lists used in school curricula: guess & check / guess & improve, act it out, draw it, make a list, look for a pattern, work backwards. NZ Maths says levels 1-2 (≈ ages 5-7) should focus on **draw, act it out, use equipment, guess & check**. Young students enjoy acting it out. Children get "stuck" on guess & check, so other strategies need deliberate promotion. Work backwards comes later. Transfer of general heuristics is historically weak (background), so they are embedded inside math content.
- **Implication:** the `TK.TOOL` strategy deck is ordered by band (G: draw / act out / use objects / guess & check. S: + list/table, pattern, smaller case, work backwards).
- Sources: [NZ Maths: problem solving strategies](https://nzmaths.co.nz/node/1231); [Kent State Pólya PDF](https://literacy.kent.edu/eureka/strategies/PolyaProcessStrategy.pdf); [LibreTexts](https://math.libretexts.org/Courses/Santa_Ana_College/Mathematics_Concepts_and_Skills_for_Elementary_School_Teachers/01%3A_Mathematical_Thinking_Problem_Solving_and_Math_as_a_Language/1.04%3A_Problem_Solving_Strategies_Part_1)

## R9. Design thinking for kids
- **Finding (M):** a 2024 review (IJTDE, ages 5-8) describes design thinking as iterative: identify goals, understand users, create solutions. It applies in three ways: as designers of real-life problems, on open-ended science problems, and inside imaginary play. K-2 classroom practice uses define → ideate → prototype → test. Evidence is practitioner-level (C).
- **Implication:** `SD.MAKER` uses a 5-step kid cycle, "למי? → מה הבעיה? → הרבה רעיונות → בונים → בודקים ומשפרים". Play-embedded briefs for K2 and real-user briefs (a family member) for K1.
- Sources: [LJMU DATE article](https://openjournals.ljmu.ac.uk/DesignTechnologyEducation/article/view/1566); [Turku review record](https://research.utu.fi/converis/portal/detail/Publication/458963852); [DRS 2016](https://drs2016.org/200)

## R11. Emotion vocabulary for kids (development, Hebrew)
- **Finding (M):** Ridgeway, Waters & Kuczaj (1985) normed 125 emotion labels for ages 18-71 months. More than 75% of 3-year-olds use *good, happy, sad, angry, loving, mean, surprised*. *Disgusted, annoyed, nervous* are typically produced closer to age 5. Vocabulary grows sharply from 4 to 11 (Baron-Cohen et al. 2010 tested 336 words in ages 4-16; the "Let's Talk About Emotions" study covered ages 4-11, but its findings were not retrieved). The MoE Hebrew-language guidance recommends an "emotion-words board" in class (מילות רגש כרטיסים). The פו"פ basics are פחד, עצב, כעס, שמחה, tied to body sensations. **No validated Hebrew child emotion-word list was found.**
- **Implication:** the spec's Hebrew lists (T: 4 words, G: 12, S: 36) are **design proposals** ordered by the English developmental norms. They need review by a Hebrew-speaking child psychologist or speech-language pathologist (open item P-02).
- Sources: [PMC9382957 record](https://ebusca.uv.mx/Record/pubmed-9382957); [Frontiers 2010 PDF](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/evolutionary-neuroscience/articles/10.3389/fnevo.2010.00109/pdf); [Springer 2021](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s42761-021-00040-2); [משרד החינוך: מניפת הרגשות](https://meyda.education.gov.il/files/Pop/0files/ivrit_hinuch_leshoni/Yesodi/menifatharegashot.pdf); [NC FIPP emotion words](https://fipp.ncdhhs.gov/wp-content/uploads/Emotion-Words-to-Use-with-Young-Children-UA.pdf)

## R12. FRIENDS and Coping Cat (FEAR) steps
- **Finding (H for FEAR, M for FRIENDS):** Coping Cat FEAR = **F**eeling frightened? (body cues) → **E**xpecting bad things? (anxious thoughts) → **A**ttitudes & actions that can help (coping talk, problem solving) → **R**esults & rewards (did I try to cope? reward the effort, not the outcome). Phase 1 teaches the skills and phase 2 practices them in exposure tasks. The FRIENDS acronym **varies by version and source**. The stable core is Feelings → Remember to Relax (quiet time) → I can try my best / helpful thoughts → Explore solutions (step plans) → Now reward yourself → Don't forget to practise → Stay calm / smile. Fun FRIENDS (ages 4-7) uses play-based wording.
- **Implication:** the spec uses its **own** Hebrew 4-step plan, "תוכנית אמ"ץ" (described in §SE.BRAVE), which is functionally FEAR-like. It does not copy branded acronyms. Effort-based reward ("ניסיתי") follows FEAR's R step.
- Sources: [Coping Cat (Wikipedia via search)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Coping_Cat); [MGH CME Henin 2021](https://mghcme.org/app/uploads/2021/03/Henin-2021_Henin_Young_Child.pdf); [FRIENDS (Wikipedia via search)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/FRIENDS_program); [PMC5102957](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5102957); [VIVE review](https://vive.dk/media/pure/mxbdkm5z/19760208); [Foundations UK: Fun FRIENDS](https://foundations.org.uk/toolkit/guidebook/fun-friends/)

## R13. Conflict-resolution scripts for young children
- **Finding (M):** Peace Table (Montessori): cool down first, use a talking piece, I-statement "When you ___, I felt ___. I would like ___", the other child restates, then both agree. It is practised when calm, not mid-fight. Kelso's Choices (K-3) offers 9 options for small problems, including wait and cool off, talk it out, share/take turns, walk away, and make a deal, with "big problems → adult". The I-message is "I feel… when… Can you please…?". Second Step K calm-down: **Stop (signal) → Name your feeling → Calm down (breathe, count, positive self-talk)**. Problem solving there begins "S: Say the problem (without blame), T: Think of solutions (safe & respectful)"; the rest of the acronym was not confirmed.
- **Implication:** `SE.TEAM.2` "שיחת צוות" uses 5 steps (stop & breathe → I-message → repeat back → 3 ideas → choose and shake on it). `SE.TEAM.1` adds a "small problem / big problem" split: a big problem (hurt, unsafe) always goes to a parent.
- Sources: [Guidepost Montessori Peace Table](https://guidepostmontessori.com/blog/elementary-series-peace-table-means-conflict-resolution); [Salem-Keizer counselor](https://sites.google.com/salkeiz.k12.or.us/schoolcounselorbenson/conflict-resolution); [Kelso's Choices (Knox)](https://www.knoxschools.org/Page/24033); [Second Step K home link](https://faulconbri-p.schools.nsw.gov.au/content/dam/doe/sws/schools/f/faulconbri-p/second-step/KL14_Home_Link.pdf); [Second Step belly breathing](https://cdn.secondstep.org/static/pdf/early-learning/second-step-free-resources-el-teaching-belly-breathing-to-my-family.pdf)

## R14. Bravery ladder design (graded exposure)
- **Finding (M):** the standard method: pick **one** avoided situation for the top rung. Add smaller steps below it. Rate each step 0-10 (for young children, adults must read how the child shows anxiety). Keep **separate ladders for separate fears**. Don't jump too fast, since that is counterproductive. If a step brings strong anxiety, **step back down** and climb again. **Invite, don't demand.** **Never desensitize a child to cues of real danger.**
- **Implication:** `SE.BRAVE` uses a 0-3 "מד-פחד" (picture thermometer) for G and 0-5 for S. Rungs are parent-co-signed. Danger topics are blocklisted, including anything siren/shelter related (B8). There is an automatic "step back" rule, and a red flag fires on repeated top fear (RF-05).
- Sources: [UW in-vivo hierarchy PDF](https://depts.washington.edu/uwhatc/wp-content/uploads/2024/11/In-Vivo-Establishing-Exposure-Hierarchy-Fear-Ladder.pdf); [UWM central therapy strategies](https://sites.uwm.edu/cnrl-feartherapy/?p=901); [Psychology Tools: Facing your fears (CYP)](https://www.psychologytools.com/tools/pdf-tools/facing-your-fears-cyp); [NE Lincs exposure ladder](https://www.nelincs.gov.uk/assets/uploads/2023/08/Anxiety-Exposure-Ladder.pdf)

## R15. Goal setting for kids (WOOP / MCII)
- **Finding (M):** Duckworth, Gollwitzer, Oettingen et al. (2013, *SPPS* 4(6):745-753): primary pupils (mostly low-income) taught mental contrasting with implementation intentions (Wish-Outcome-Obstacle-Plan) improved grades, attendance and behaviour **compared with a positive-thinking control**. Vocabulary-recall studies in German and US elementary and middle schools found mental contrasting beat fantasizing. A 2022 *Nature Human Behaviour* study found that a self-regulation unit in **first grade** raised the likelihood of an advanced secondary track three years later. Caveats: no neutral control in some studies, and effect sizes were not retrieved.
- **Implication:** `SD.GOAL.2` (K1) uses a kid WOOP, "חלום → הכי טוב → מה עלול להפריע → אם… אז…". Evidence is graded B, the strongest in strand 4. G band gets only "if-then" plans with a parent.
- Sources: [Nature Hum Behav 2022](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41562-022-01449-w); [KQED MindShift](https://www.kqed.org/mindshift/38827/why-understanding-obstacles-is-essential-to-achieving-goals); [Schools Week](https://schoolsweek.co.uk/how-can-we-help-students-to-commit-to-action-then-act-on-their-commitment); [Panorama WOOP](https://panoramaed.com/blog/setting-goals-woop)

## R16. Entrepreneurship activities for kids
- **Finding (M):** the evidence is thin. A Quebec quasi-experiment (ages 10-12, Pepin & St-Jean) found **no overall difference** from single entrepreneurial projects. Repeated projects had a small positive effect on 3 of 4 attitudes. A Mexican 6th-grade case found children most engaged by **naming products and designing posters** (marketing). Polish "Entrepreneurial Kids" (ages 3-10) is descriptive only. No JA BizTown evaluation was retrieved.
- **Implication:** entrepreneurship is a C-graded *flavour* of `SD.MAKER.3`, not a strand. It recurs across the year rather than as a single event (Quebec finding). Children lead with what they enjoy: naming, poster, pitch to family. No real money for K2. For K1, real money only by parent choice, outside the app (D1: no real money in app).
- Sources: [Emerald JSBED Pepin & St-Jean](https://emerald.com/insight/content/doi/10.1108/JSBED-07-2018-0224/full/html); [DOAJ Ra Ximhai 2025](https://doaj.org/article/5919cf78eb1d46fe82cf5ab0a5c6f574); [MDPI Educ Sci](https://mdpi.com/2227-7102/13/9/894)
