# Future-Ready Thinking Toolkit and Dispositions for Ages 2-10 (Evidence-Graded, with Game Mechanics)

Scope note: Covers the thinking toolkit, dispositions and how to teach them. AI-literacy curricula, AI-tutor evidence and OECD/PISA frameworks belong to other researchers. Research date: Nov 2026. Network note: www.weforum.org could not be reached directly from this session (DNS blocked), so the WEF figures below come from search snippets of the WEF report PDF and WEF pages plus secondary coverage. Treat exact percentages as "verify against PDF before print".

Evidence grades used throughout:
- **A**: multiple RCTs or meta-analyses with consistent positive effects
- **B**: some RCTs or meta-analyses, but mixed, small, or not yet replicated at scale
- **C**: theory, practitioner frameworks, quasi-experimental or self-selected studies only
- **D**: myth, or contradicted by better evidence

## 1. What do the latest reports say about which skills rise and which decline as AI automates knowledge work?

### Takeaway
Employer surveys (WEF 2025) put human cognitive-dispositional skills at the top of the "core skills" list: analytical thinking, resilience/flexibility/agility, leadership and social influence, creative thinking, self-awareness, curiosity and lifelong learning. They also name AI/big data and tech literacy as the fastest-growing skills. Usage data from AI labs (Anthropic Economic Index 2026) show people increasingly *collaborate* with AI (augmentation) rather than just delegate to it. Brookings (Jan 2026) warns that AI can currently displace children's thinking effort, and that families are the front line. Together these point to a toolkit built on thinking for yourself, judgement, and choosing tools.

### Cited Findings
**WEF Future of Jobs Report 2025 (Jan 2025)**
- Analytical thinking remains the top core skill, and seven in ten companies consider it essential — [WEF Skills Outlook ch.3](https://www.weforum.org/publications/the-future-of-jobs-report-2025/in-full/3-skills-outlook/); [WEF FoJ 2025 PDF](https://reports.weforum.org/docs/WEF_Future_of_Jobs_Report_2025.pdf)
- Resilience, flexibility and agility was cited by 67% of employers, second to analytical thinking at 69% — [Lexology summary of WEF FoJ 2025](https://www.lexology.com/library/detail.aspx?g=b1de1623-e5ab-471f-9d53-b148efc216a3); [HR Online](https://www.humanresourcesonline.net/most-in-demand-core-skills-globally-in-2025-2030)
- The top-10 core skills are: analytical thinking; resilience, flexibility and agility; leadership and social influence; creative thinking; motivation and self-awareness; technological literacy; empathy and active listening; curiosity and lifelong learning (about 50%); talent management; service orientation — [Lexology](https://www.lexology.com/library/detail.aspx?g=b1de1623-e5ab-471f-9d53-b148efc216a3); [Skillsoft summary](https://www.skillsoft.com/blog/the-top-10-skills-rising-in-importance-according-to-1-000-employers)
- The fastest-growing skills to 2030 are AI and big data, then networks and cybersecurity, then technological literacy. Creative thinking, resilience/flexibility/agility, and curiosity and lifelong learning are also rising in importance. Environmental stewardship entered the top-10 fastest-growing for the first time — [WEF Skills Outlook](https://www.weforum.org/publications/the-future-of-jobs-report-2025/in-full/3-skills-outlook/); [EU Digital Skills & Jobs Platform](https://digital-skills-jobs.europa.eu/en/latest/news/great-skills-reset-wefs-future-jobs-report-2025-catch-22-future-work)
- Macro picture: 170M jobs created and 92M displaced by 2030 (net +78M) — [ThisDayLive](https://www.thisdaylive.com/2025/01/10/wef-report-shifting-global-trends-to-generate-170m-new-jobs-by-2030-displace-92m-others/); [WION](https://www.wionews.com/world/world-economic-forum-wef-report-78-million-new-jobs-added-by-2030-led-by-ai-big-data-and-cybersecurity-8602988)

**Brookings Global Task Force, "A New Direction for Students in an AI World: Prosper, Prepare, Protect" (Jan 2026)**
- Authors: Burns, Winthrop, Luther, Venetis and Karim (Center for Universal Education). The report draws on a year-long "premortem" with 505 participants in 50 countries — [Brookings PDF mirror (GMU)](https://webdocs.gmu.edu/wp-content/uploads/A-New-Direction-for-Students-in-an-AI-World.pdf); [babl.ai summary](https://babl.ai/brookings-report-warns-ais-expanding-role-in-childhood-education-carries-growing-risks-without-guardrails/)
- Headline: at present the risks of AI in education outweigh its benefits, but the trajectory "is not yet fixed". Its framework rests on three pillars: Prosper, Prepare, Protect — [ETUCE](https://www.csee-etuce.org/en/item/7153:new-brookings-report-warns-risks-of-ai-in-education-currently-outweigh-the-benefits); [Benton](https://www.benton.org/headlines/new-direction-students-ai-world-prosper-prepare-protect)
- AI can displace cognitive effort. Teachers report declines in reading and writing (which the report frames as core to deep thinking), plus "digitally induced amnesia". Students use AI more outside school than inside, which raises "artificial intimacy" concerns. Families, not schools, are the front line — [Brookings PDF mirror](https://webdocs.gmu.edu/wp-content/uploads/A-New-Direction-for-Students-in-an-AI-World.pdf); [Fortune, Jan 2026](https://fortune.com/2026/01/15/ai-brainrot-students-brookings-study-cheating-high-school-fast-food/); [Fortune, Feb 2026, teachers on reasoning](https://fortune.com/2026/02/24/students-cant-reason-teachers-warn-ai-fueling-crisis-in-kids-ability-to-think/). Note that terms like "cognitive debt" and "brainrot" are Fortune's framing, not necessarily the report's language.
- Critical review: NEPC (Colorado) reviewed it and called it cautionary — [NEPC](https://www.nepc.colorado.edu/node/14179)

**Anthropic Economic Index (how AI is actually used in work)**
- Jan 2026 report ("Economic primitives"): on Claude.ai, augmentation (52% of conversations) overtook automation (45%), up 5pp from Nov 2025. Augmentation is split into *learning* (asking for explanations), *task iteration* (collaborating) and *validation* (asking for feedback). Automation is *directive* use or a *feedback loop* — [Anthropic, Jan 2026](https://www.anthropic.com/research/anthropic-economic-index-january-2026-report)
- API (enterprise) traffic is much more automation-heavy. An earlier report found ~77% automation patterns, mostly directive — [Anthropic primitives](https://www.anthropic.com/research/economic-index-primitives) (the exact report vintage is unconfirmed)
- June 2026 "Cadences" report (survey launched April 2026): respondents reported gains in speed (86%), scope (82%) and quality (69%). 68% say they learn more with AI, and 57% say AI made their skills more valuable — [Anthropic, June 2026](https://www.anthropic.com/research/economic-index-june-2026-report)

### Inferences
- For the app, the WEF top-10 maps almost one-to-one onto teachable dispositions: analytical thinking becomes the reasoning and problem-solving toolkit. Resilience/flexibility becomes productive struggle plus strategy switching. Curiosity/lifelong learning becomes question quests. Leadership/social influence plus empathy become collaboration and persuasion missions. Creative thinking becomes invent challenges. Self-awareness becomes metacognition and reflection.
- The Anthropic augmentation categories (learning, iteration, validation) amount to a ready-made "how to work with a tool" vocabulary for kids aged 8+: *ask it to explain*, *build together*, *check my work*. These are the healthy modes. Pure "do it for me" (directive) is the mode Brookings warns displaces thinking. A game could reward "I tried first, then checked with a tool" over "the tool did it".
- Brookings' framing (families are the front line, AI displaces effort) argues that the app's AI features should be *scaffold-then-fade* and *think-first*. It also argues for strong non-screen, offline-transfer components.
- WEF data is an employer-opinion survey about 2030. It is a weak predictor of what matters in 2040-2050, when these children enter work. The robust bet is on *durable meta-skills* (learning how to learn, judgement, self-regulation, collaboration) rather than specific tech skills.

### Gaps
- Could not access weforum.org directly to verify (a) the "39% of skills will change by 2030" figure (background recall, unverified), (b) the declining-skills list (background recall: "manual dexterity, endurance and precision" shows the largest net decline; reading/writing/maths and global citizenship are often listed as stable or declining). Verify both against the WEF PDF.
- Did not cover McKinsey, LinkedIn or OpenAI skill reports, OECD "Trends Shaping Education 2025", Stanford d.school K-12 or Harvard Project Zero's AI-era statements (these are out of scope or ran out of tool budget).

## 2. Which problem-solving and thinking toolkits are teachable to young children, and how strong is the evidence?

### Takeaway
The best-evidenced items are **metacognition and self-regulation strategies** (EEF: +7 months, strong evidence) and **computational thinking** for ages 3-8 (meta-analytic d ~0.6-1.0, though from small studies). **Productive failure** has meta-analytic support but may favour instruction-first for younger grades. Popular branded programs are much weaker than their reputations: **Tools of the Mind** (pre-K RCTs mostly null), **Philosophy for Children** (positive efficacy trial, not confirmed at scale), **Project Zero Thinking Routines** (practitioner-strong, evidence thin) and **HighScope plan-do-review** (program-level evidence only). Use the frameworks as *content and rituals*, embedded in subject tasks, not as stand-alone "thinking skills lessons".

### Cited Findings
**Metacognition / self-regulated learning (Grade A)**
- EEF Toolkit: metacognition and self-regulation is worth about +7 months of additional progress (revised down from +8 when newer studies were added). It is rated high-impact, low-cost, with 4 padlocks — [EEF Toolkit update blog](https://educationendowmentfoundation.org.uk/news/latest-updates-to-the-toolkit/); [EEF guidance news](https://educationendowmentfoundation.org.uk/news/new-guidance-on-how-to-develop-pupils-metacognitive-skills/)
- EEF guidance report (Quigley, Muijs, Stringer; 2nd ed. 2021) gives seven recommendations, including explicitly teaching metacognitive strategies (plan, monitor, evaluate), teachers *modelling* their own thinking, and setting an appropriate level of *challenge*. A key "myth-bust" is that metacognition should be taught with subject content, **not** in separate "learning to learn" or "thinking skills" lessons — [EEF](https://educationendowmentfoundation.org.uk/news/new-guidance-on-how-to-develop-pupils-metacognitive-skills/); [SecEd](https://www.sec-ed.co.uk/news/how-to-unlock-the-learning-potential-of-metacognition/); [Hampshire copy of report](https://english.hias.hants.gov.uk/mod/resource/view.php?id=1997)

**Tools of the Mind, a Vygotskian executive-function curriculum (Grade B-/mixed, mostly null in pre-K)**
- Positive: Blair & Raver (2014) ran a cluster RCT of the kindergarten version (29 schools, 79 classrooms, 759 children). It found positive effects on executive function, reasoning, attention control and stress-hormone (cortisol/alpha-amylase) profiles, plus maths gains (WJ-III Applied Problems). Reading did not reach significance — [PMC: Blair & Raver 2014](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4229187); [Evidence for ESSA](https://evidenceforessa.org/?p=692)
- Null: Farran's Tennessee pre-K RCT found no effects on literacy, language, maths or self-regulation gains — [Farran AERA 2013 slides](https://cdn.vanderbilt.edu/vu-my/wp-content/uploads/sites/412/2011/12/14084326/Farran-AERA-PRESENTATION-4-28-13.pdf). The SRCD Monograph (Nesbitt & Farran 2021) followed children to the end of 1st grade and found no positive effects on academic, EF, self-regulation or social outcomes. The Head Start CARES trial was also null for Tools on EF — [SRCD Monograph Matters](https://monographmatters.srcd.org/?p=2227); [Stanford DREME](https://dreme.stanford.edu/?p=1727); [PMC 2021 review](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC7986366)

**Philosophy for Children / Socratic dialogue (Grade B-)**
- EEF efficacy trial (Gorard, Siddiqui & See, 2015; 3,159 pupils, 48 schools, rated 3/5 padlocks) found about +2 months in KS2 reading and maths, with larger gains for disadvantaged pupils (reading +4, maths +3 months). Critics noted the absence of significance testing — [EEF project page](https://educationendowmentfoundation.org.uk/projects-and-evaluation/projects/philosophy-for-children); [EEF report](https://dera.ioe.ac.uk//32011/1/EEF_Project_Report_PhilosophyForChildren.pdf); [Schools Week](https://schoolsweek.co.uk/philosophy-sessions-improve-pupil-learning)
- The larger NFER effectiveness trial (Lord et al., 2021; about 200 schools planned) found *no evidence of impact on social and communication skills*. I could not confirm its KS2 attainment result from snippets — [NFER](https://www.nfer.ac.uk/publications/evaluation-of-philosophy-for-children-an-effectiveness-trial/); [ISRCTN registry](https://www.isrctn.com/pdf/ISRCTN11118203)

**Harvard Project Zero Thinking Routines / Visible Thinking (Grade C)**
- Searches found no rigorous outcomes RCT. The available studies are framework papers and small classroom trials (e.g., Malaysian and Spanish primary implementations), plus publisher claims of positive engagement effects — [UiTM Malaysia study](https://ir.uitm.edu.my/id/eprint/43284); [Univ. Valladolid thesis](https://uvadoc.uva.es/bitstream/handle/10324/22985/PARADA%20RODR%c3%8dGUEZ%2c%20NEREA.pdf?sequence=1); [publisher claim](https://www.everand.com/book/575606708/The-Power-of-Making-Thinking-Visible-Practices-to-Engage-and-Empower-All-Learners)

**HighScope Plan-Do-Review (Grade C for the component; program-level evidence via Perry Preschool)**
- One small Thai pre/post study (30 kindergarteners, 8 weeks, 5x/week) reported EF gains. It had no clear control group — [Burapha Univ.](https://buuir.buu.ac.th/bitstream/1234567890/4664/1/edu32n3p56-70.pdf)
- Long-term evidence is for the HighScope curriculum *as a whole* (Perry Preschool), not for isolated plan-do-review. Mechanism claims (metacognitive cycle builds EF) are largely theoretical — [summary](https://www.drmattlynch.com/what-are-highsc)

**Computational thinking for young children (Grade B+)**
- Meta-analysis of ages 3-8: 17 studies, 1,665 children, d = 0.83, moderated by scaffolding and education level — [Springer/ResearchGate record via search](https://link.springer.com/content/pdf/10.1007/s10639-021-10700-2.pdf); [ERIC record](https://discovery-imamu.kwaretech.com/EdsRecord/eric,EJ1365559)
- Unplugged (no screen) CT activities for K-9: g = 0.63 across 15 studies — [K-9 unplugged meta-analysis](https://vufind-test.katalog.k.utb.cz/SummonRecord/FETCH-LOGICAL-c358t-e9bef564135f179e6073c16a124d28e9ac8dbbed29e2012ea2673cede1388c3e3). Another unplugged meta-analysis (13 studies) found g = 1.03 — [dergipark](https://dergipark.org.tr/en/download/article-file/3868320)
- A Tufts (DevTech, Bers lab) longitudinal coding curriculum for grades 1-2, measured with the unplugged TechCheck assessment, found clear gains in grade 1 and weaker ones in grade 2 — [BC DevTech PDF](https://sites.bc.edu/devtech/wp-content/uploads/sites/181/2023/10/1-s2.0-S0360131521000993-main.pdf)

**Scientific thinking and exploration (Gopnik / Schulz) (Grade B for the descriptive science; it is basic research, not an intervention)**
- Gopnik (2020, Phil Trans R Soc B) argues that a long, protected childhood is an evolutionary solution to the explore-exploit trade-off: children explore broadly, adults exploit — [Royal Society](https://royalsocietypublishing.org/rstb/article/375/1803/20190502/31298/Childhood-as-a-solution-to-explore-exploit)
- In causal "blicket" tasks, children infer unusual hypotheses that adults miss, partly because adults stop experimenting early. Children explore more when evidence is confounded or violates expectations (Schulz & Bonawitz 2007). Schulz proposes that play is "competence exploration": learning which plans solve which class of problems — [PMC review: Structure & Development of Explore-Exploit](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11275514/); [Frontiers key concepts](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/developmental-psychology/articles/10.3389/fdpys.2024.1467880/xml)
- Caveat: some studies find no child-adult difference in random exploration (Schulz et al. 2019), and it is debated whether children's extra exploration is intrinsic motivation or distraction by novelty — [PMC review](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11275514/)
- Gopnik (Berkeley talk, Apr 2026): "AI is no match for a 4-year-old" at exploratory causal learning — [Berkeley News](https://news.berkeley.edu/2026/04/17/berkeley-talks-alison-gopnik/)

**Productive failure / problem-solving-before-instruction (Grade B+, with an age caveat)**
- Sinha & Kapur (2021, Review of Educational Research): 53 studies, 166 comparisons, more than 12,000 participants. Overall g = 0.36 [0.20, 0.51] for conceptual knowledge, with no harm to procedural knowledge. Effects reach about 0.58 when designs closely follow PF principles — [Sinha & Kapur PDF](https://janfasen.nl/wp-content/uploads/2023/05/Sinha-and-Kapur-PS-I.pdf); [ResearchGate](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/352523653_When_Problem_Solving_Followed_by_Instruction_Works_Evidence_for_Productive_Failure)
- Adding scaffolds to the initial problem-solving phase generally did not help (g = -0.08) — [ScienceDirect replication](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S0959475221000475)
- Age caveat (secondary source, needs verification): for grades 2-5 the effects reportedly *favoured instruction first* — [Mindomax blog](https://www.mindomax.com/productive-failure)

### Inferences
- **Design rule 1:** embed thinking tools *inside* content tasks (maths puzzles, nature quests, stories), never as an abstract "thinking class". This follows the EEF myth-bust and the transfer literature generally.
- **Design rule 2:** treat named frameworks (Habits of Mind, Six Hats, TRIZ, Project Zero routines) as *vocabulary and rituals*, not as proven interventions. They are good for giving kids names for thinking moves. They are not evidence that the brand itself causes gains.
- **Design rule 3:** for ages 2-6, "struggle first" should be short and playful, with fast hints. For ages 8+, longer "try-before-teach" puzzles are justified (PF evidence is strongest in older students; the grades 2-5 caveat suggests caution).
- **Executive-function lesson from Tools of the Mind:** a whole-curriculum EF approach did not reliably transfer in pre-K. An app should not promise EF gains. It can use make-believe role-play with planning (the Tools "play plan") as an engaging ritual, with modest expectations.
- **Age-graded toolkit proposal** (my synthesis; grade attached):

| Tool | 2.5 y | 5.5 y | 8.5 y | Grade |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Plan-Do-Review (metacognition) | "What will you play?" (pick 1 of 3 pictures) then "What did you do?" | Draw or say a plan, then a 1-question review ("what was tricky?") | Written or voice plan with a prediction, then "what would I change?" | A (metacognition) / C (PDR component) |
| Notice-Wonder (See-Think-Wonder lite) | "What do you see?" naming game | See / Think / Wonder on a picture | Claim-Support-Question on a "fact" | C |
| Experiment-to-find-out (scientific thinking) | Cause-effect toys, "what happens if?" | "Blicket detector" games: test which block lights it | Fair-test design: change one thing at a time | B (basic science) |
| Pólya 4 steps: Understand / Plan / Do / Look back | n/a | Picture cards: "What's the problem? Try! Check!" | Full four steps, plus heuristics: draw it, try a smaller case, work backwards, guess and check | C (heuristics) / A via metacognition |
| Computational thinking (sequence, loop, debug) | Sequencing 2-3 steps (morning routine) | Unplugged robot/arrow games, "debug" a wrong path | ScratchJr/Scratch, decompose a task, find the pattern | B+ |
| Systems thinking (cause loops) | n/a | "What happens next if…" chains (food chain) | Loop drawings ("more rabbits, then more foxes, then fewer rabbits") | C |
| Socratic/P4C dialogue | "Why?" turns | Picture-book inquiry: "Is it fair?" | Community-of-inquiry rules: give a reason, agree/disagree respectfully | B- |
| Perspective hats (de Bono-lite) | n/a | Feelings hat vs. idea hat | Full 6 hats for a family decision | C (thin) |

### Gaps
- No research found in this session on the efficacy of **Habits of Mind** (Costa & Kallick), **de Bono Six Hats/CoRT**, **TRIZ for kids**, **Waters Center systems thinking**, or **Stanford d.school K-12 design thinking** with young children. From background knowledge the evidence for each is predominantly practitioner and case-study (Grade C), but that is unverified here.
- Could not verify the Ritchhart & Perkins (2008) Educational Psychology Review paper on Visible Thinking outcomes.
- Diamond et al. 2007 (Science), an early positive Tools of the Mind study, was not retrieved. Background recall is that it was positive for EF in a small sample.
- The NFER P4C 2021 primary KS2 attainment result was not retrieved.

## 3. "Choosing the right tool": what does research say about strategy selection, adaptive expertise and transfer?

### Takeaway
Children naturally hold *several* strategies at once and shift how often they use each (Siegler's overlapping waves). Development means adaptive *selection* plus discovery of new strategies, not stage jumps. Hatano & Inagaki's adaptive expertise (being efficient *and* able to invent new procedures) needs conceptual understanding, variability of problems, and a safe environment for risk-taking. A "toolbox" game mechanic in which kids pick among several strategies, compare them and reflect on fit is well aligned with this theory. The direct intervention evidence I could verify is thinner than the theory.

### Cited Findings
- Siegler's overlapping waves: children hold multiple ways of thinking about a problem at once, choose adaptively among them, and frequently generate new ones. Development is a gradual ebb and flow of strategy frequencies, not discrete stages — [Siegler NIH grant abstract](https://grantome.com/grant/NIH/R01-HD019011-13); [Siegler chapter draft, Erikson](https://earlymath.erikson.edu/wp-content/uploads/2016/10/Siegler-draft-10_25_16.pdf); [Science News "Learning in waves"](https://www.sciencenews.org/article/learning-waves)
- The pattern appears even in infants. For example, infants use multiple descent strategies on slopes within and across sessions — [Databrary video](https://nyu.databrary.org/volume/471). Siegler's SCADS computational model covers strategy choice plus discovery — [ERIC ED501538](https://files.eric.ed.gov/fulltext/ED501538.pdf)
- Strategy choice is defined as selecting an appropriate strategy from one's repertoire — [Springer Instructional Science 2018](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s11251-018-9457-1)
- Overlapping waves have also been used to model *tool-use* development in robotics — [Forestier & Oudeyer 2016](http://www.pyoudeyer.com/forestierOudeyerICDLEpirob16.pdf)
- Hatano & Inagaki (1986) contrast routine expertise (efficient, accurate execution of known procedures) with adaptive expertise (efficiently solving familiar tasks *and* generating new procedures for novel ones). Adaptive expertise requires conceptual as well as procedural understanding. Supporting conditions include *variability/random context*, *a safe environment for risk-taking*, and *valuing understanding* — [Wikipedia: Adaptive expertise](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Adaptive_expertise); [Springer Adv Health Sci Educ 2022](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s10459-022-10190-y); [Lancashire review](https://knowledge.lancashire.ac.uk/id/eprint/31616/1/31616%20Adv%20expt%20CLOK%20version.pdf)
- Adaptive expertise may be domain-specific, built through accumulated experiences — [Frontline Learning Research 2024](https://flr.journals.publicknowledgeproject.org/index.php/journal/article/download/543/1247?inline=1)
- Related: productive-failure evidence (section 2) suggests that generating multiple solution attempts *before* instruction improves conceptual understanding and transfer (g = 0.36) — [Sinha & Kapur 2021](https://janfasen.nl/wp-content/uploads/2023/05/Sinha-and-Kapur-PS-I.pdf)
- Exploration as "competence exploration" (Schulz): play teaches which plans solve which class of problems, which is in effect learning to choose tools — [PMC review](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11275514/)

### Inferences
- **"Toolbox" mechanic, theory-aligned:** give the child 2-4 explicit "tools" per domain (maths: count-on, make-ten, draw-it, guess-and-check; reading: picture clue, sound it out, reread; life: ask someone, look it up, try it, ask AI). After solving, ask "Which tool did you use? Was there a faster one?" This mirrors overlapping waves (multiple strategies coexist) and builds selection through comparison.
- **Interleave problem types** so the tool is not cued by the chapter (variability, per Hatano). A problem set that mixes "count it / measure it / ask someone / experiment" forces selection.
- **"Tool-fit" meta-question for the AI age (ages 7+):** "Is this a job for my brain, a ruler, a book, a grown-up, or an AI?" Pair it with the Anthropic augmentation modes (explain / build together / check my work).
- **Safe-to-fail:** Hatano's "safe environment for risk-taking" supports no-penalty retries and celebration of strategy experiments.
- **Age grading:** at 2.5, choosing between two physical tools (spoon vs. scoop; stack vs. push) and trial and error is the toolkit. At 5.5, 2-3 named strategies with picture icons. At 8.5, a full strategy deck, "efficiency races" between strategies, and inventing a new tool.

### Gaps
- Could not retrieve Rittle-Johnson & Star's comparison studies (comparing multiple solution methods improves procedural flexibility). Background knowledge says this is a solid line of RCTs in middle-school maths, but it is unverified here, and the evidence in ages 5-8 is less clear.
- No direct RCT was found of a "toolbox"/"which strategy fits?" intervention for ages 2-10. The mechanic rests on theory plus metacognition evidence.
- Transfer of general problem-solving heuristics (Pólya-style) across domains is historically weak in the literature (background knowledge; not verified this session).

## 4. Dispositions: curiosity, intellectual humility, agency, grit/passion, challenge-seeking, creativity, collaboration

### Takeaway
Dispositions matter, but the "hype" constructs are weaker than claimed. **Grit** is largely conscientiousness and predicts performance only modestly (perseverance matters more than "passion consistency"). **Growth-mindset interventions** show near-zero average effects on achievement, with possible benefits for at-risk subgroups. The **"NASA 98% creative geniuses" study** is unverifiable folklore, though a real (contested) decline in Torrance scores since about 1990, largest in K-3, was reported by Kim (2011). **Curiosity** episodes drop sharply from home to school and from kindergarten to grade 5. **Intellectual humility** is recognized and valued by about age 5.5. The design implication is to build curiosity, agency and challenge-seeking through *environmental structure* (choice, questions rewarded, safe failure) rather than through lectures on mindset or grit.

### Cited Findings
**Grit (Grade D for the "grit" construct as a lever; B for perseverance)**
- Credé, Tynan & Harms (2017, JPSP): 584 effect sizes, 88 samples, N = 66,807. Grit correlates only moderately with performance (about r = .18) and retention. It is very strongly correlated with conscientiousness, and its higher-order structure is not confirmed. The perseverance-of-effort facet has better validity than consistency of interests and explains some variance beyond conscientiousness. Grit interventions "may only have weak effects" — [PubMed](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/27845531/); [Credé PDF via Gwern](https://www.Gwern.net/doc/psychology/personality/conscientiousness/2016-crede.pdf); [Scott Barry Kaufman summary](https://scottbarrykaufman.com/study-alert-much-ado-grit-meta-analytic-synthesis-grit-literature/)

**Growth mindset (Grade B-/contested)**
- Macnamara & Burgoyne (2023, Psych Bulletin), 63 studies: apparent effects are attributable to study design flaws and bias. The best-evidence subset gives d = 0.02 [-0.06, 0.10] — [APA manuscript](https://psycnet.apa.org/manuscript/2023-90931-003.pdf); [Macnamara PDF](https://alexanderpburgoyne.com/wp-content/uploads/2025/02/Macnamara-et-al.-2022-Do-Growth-Mindset-Interventions-Impact-Students-Academic-Achievement.pdf)
- Burnette et al. (2023) used multilevel meta-regression and found meaningful effects in focal (at-risk) groups. Tipton, Bryan et al. argued for heterogeneity-focused methods, and Macnamara & Burgoyne replied disputing them — [Tipton et al. commentary](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/Why-Meta-Analyses-of-Growth-Mindset-and-Other-Best-Tipton-Bryan/be3d59f054b129064fe86b86bcbfdf88e37c7a5c); [Reply](https://gwern.net/doc/statistics/meta-analysis/2023-macnamara.pdf)

**Creativity (the myth is Grade D; the decline trend is Grade C)**
- "NASA test, 1,600 children, 98% creative genius at age 5, 2% as adults" (George Land & Beth Jarman, *Breakpoint and Beyond*, 1992): NASA reportedly told an inquirer it had no record of such a project with Land, and Emil Kirkegaard found no trace of the research. The figures vary across retellings (30% at age 10 vs. 12% at age 15). Treat it as unverified folklore and do not cite it — [Squarespace "Creative myth 2"](https://oleander-sailfish-4hpx.squarespace.com/insights/creative-myth-2-children-are-more-creative-than-adults); [example of uncritical retelling](https://amandastern.substack.com/p/nasastudy). (The debunk itself relies on secondary sources; no peer-reviewed debunk was found.)
- Kim (2011, Creativity Research Journal) analysed about 300,000 Torrance (TTCT) results from 1968-2008. Scores declined from about 1990 while IQ rose, and the decline was most significant for kindergarten to grade 3. Kim attributes it to less free play and more screens, which is interpretive, and TTCT validity is contested — [William & Mary](https://www.wm.edu/research/news/social-sciences/smart-yes-creative-not-so-much.php); [ResearchGate](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/254301584_The_Creativity_Crisis_The_Decrease_in_Creative_Thinking_Scores_on_the_Torrance_Tests_of_Creative_Thinking); [Newsweek 2010](https://www.newsweek.com/creativity-crisis-74665); [Inc. noting debate](https://www.inc.com/jessica-stillman/creativity-crisis-torrance-test.html)
- Gray links the decline of children's free play and freedom to declining creativity. This is correlational argument — [Psychology Today, Peter Gray](https://www.psychologytoday.com/us/blog/freedom-learn/201209/children-s-freedom-has-declined-so-has-their-creativity)

**Curiosity (Grade B for descriptive data)**
- Engel: "episodes of curiosity" (questions, manipulating objects, directed gazing) occurred 2.36 times per 2 hours in kindergarten vs. 0.48 in grade 5 — [Engel 2011, "Children's Need to Know"](https://elimindset.com/wp-content/uploads/2022/08/engel2011.pdf); [Engel, Penn](https://cpb-us-w2.wpmucdn.com/web.sas.upenn.edu/dist/8/471/files/2018/06/Susan_Engel-1l0il50.pdf)
- Tizard & Hughes found that children asked about 26 questions per hour at home vs. about 2 per hour at school. The "107 questions/hour" figure (Chouinard 2007) comes from a tiny sample of 4 children — [Psychology Today "Are kids curious?"](https://www.psychologytoday.com/us/blog/this-is-america/201502/are-kids-curious); [Ness Labs](https://nesslabs.com/science-of-curiosity)
- Young children's question-asking is limited by their ability to identify categorical features for *constraint-seeking* questions (vs. hypothesis-scanning). This can be scaffolded — [Frontiers in Psychology 2020](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/psychology/articles/10.3389/fpsyg.2020.586819/full)
- A pedagogical-agent study prompted children to generate convergent vs. divergent questions (small within-subject design) — [arXiv 2004.03472](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2004.03472)

**Intellectual humility (Grade B- descriptive; intervention evidence absent)**
- Children by about age 5.5 prefer intellectually humble over arrogant people, and the preference strengthens with age (2 studies, N = 229, Developmental Psychology 2025) — [PubMed 40388175](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/40388175/); [NSF PAR](https://par.nsf.gov/biblio/10650207-do-children-value-intellectual-humility-over-intellectual-arrogance)
- Young children overestimate their knowledge even while showing humble behaviours — [Springer Current Psychology 2025 review](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s12144-025-08608-y)
- Among 6-10-year-olds, kids low in intellectual humility resist feedback, while kids high in it are very sensitive to feedback — [Barnes, U. Arkansas](https://scholarworks.uark.edu/etd/5504/)
- How parents talk about uncertainty may shape children's intellectual humility. However, parent self-reported humility did not correlate with behavioural humility, and higher self-reported parent humility was linked to *less* humble children (N = 108) — [PsyPost](https://www.psypost.org/how-parents-talk-about-uncertainty-may-shape-childrens-intellectual-humility/); [PubMed 40372902](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/40372902/)
- Intellectual humility relates to error-monitoring neural markers (ERN/Pe) and intelligence in children — [ResearchGate](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/319896767_Intelligence_and_Neurophysiological_Markers_of_Error_Monitoring_Relate_to_Children's_Intellectual_Humility)

**Challenge-seeking / productive struggle**
- Productive failure evidence (section 2): g = 0.36, with an age caveat — [Sinha & Kapur 2021](https://janfasen.nl/wp-content/uploads/2023/05/Sinha-and-Kapur-PS-I.pdf)
- EEF lists "set an appropriate level of challenge" as a core metacognition recommendation — [EEF](https://educationendowmentfoundation.org.uk/news/new-guidance-on-how-to-develop-pupils-metacognitive-skills/)
- Hatano names a safe environment for risk-taking as a condition for adaptive expertise — [Wikipedia: Adaptive expertise](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Adaptive_expertise)

### Inferences
- **Do not build a "grit meter" or a growth-mindset lecture.** Instead, build *perseverance through structure*: visible progress on hard tasks, praise for strategy and effort-plus-strategy ("you tried a new tool!"), and choice of challenge level. This is consistent with Credé (perseverance facet) and with the weak average effects of mindset interventions.
- **Passion should be explored, not demanded.** Since "consistency of interests" is the weaker facet, young kids *should* sample widely. That also fits Gopnik's explore-before-exploit view of childhood. Ambition for ages 2-10 should look like "explore many, go deep on a few for a season".
- **Curiosity is fragile and environment-dependent.** The app should *reward questions*, not only answers ("question coins"). It should also scaffold good questions: 20-questions-style "guess who" games train constraint-seeking questions.
- **Intellectual humility vocabulary** ("I'm not sure yet", "I changed my mind because…", "How could I find out?") is teachable through modelling. Parental modelling may need to be *behavioural* (the app prompts the parent to say "I don't know, let's check") rather than self-perception.
- **Creativity:** avoid the NASA myth in marketing. Do use the Kim data cautiously to motivate protecting unstructured, open-ended play and divergent-thinking tasks ("how many uses for a cup?").
- **Agency / self-direction:** offer choices at every level (what quest, which tool, how hard), plus kid-set goals for ages 7+. This is partially grounded in self-determination theory, which was not researched here (see Gaps).

### Gaps
- Not retrieved this session: Engel's *The Hungry Mind* (2015) specifics; Loewenstein's information-gap theory; Stixrud & Johnson's *The Self-Driven Child* (a popular book; underlying sense-of-control research not verified); self-determination theory meta-analyses for autonomy support; Bjork's desirable difficulties with young children; Duckworth's grit-in-kids data; Junior Achievement and kid-entrepreneurship program evaluations; healthy vs. unhealthy perfectionism in children (Curran & Hill 2019 reported rising perfectionism, unverified here); collaboration and persuasion training for young children.
- No RCT was found showing a curiosity-raising intervention in typical classrooms, or that intellectual humility training improves learning.
- The Land & Jarman debunk rests on secondary sources (NASA inquiry by Elisabeth McClure; Emil Kirkegaard investigation). No primary documentation was found either way.

## 5. Concrete, age-graded game mechanics (2.5 / 5.5 / 8.5) to teach the toolkit and dispositions

### Takeaway
The most defensible mechanics are those that **(a)** embed plan-monitor-evaluate cycles into every session (metacognition, Grade A), **(b)** reward trying several strategies and comparing them (overlapping waves / adaptive expertise, theory-strong), **(c)** make challenge opt-in and failure safe (productive failure / Hatano), **(d)** reward questions and experiments (curiosity / Gopnik-Schulz), and **(e)** push transfer to the offline world, because Brookings warns that AI and screens displace effort and families are the front line.

### Cited Findings
(Mechanics are design inferences. The evidence each rests on is cited.)
- The plan-monitor-evaluate cycle, modelling, and challenge are EEF's core recommendations (+7 months) — [EEF](https://educationendowmentfoundation.org.uk/news/new-guidance-on-how-to-develop-pupils-metacognitive-skills/)
- HighScope's Plan-Do-Review is a well-established early-years ritual. Component-level evidence is thin; program-level evidence comes from Perry Preschool — [Burapha study](https://buuir.buu.ac.th/bitstream/1234567890/4664/1/edu32n3p56-70.pdf); [HighScope summary](https://www.drmattlynch.com/what-are-highsc)
- Multiple strategies coexist and get selected adaptively — [Siegler](https://grantome.com/grant/NIH/R01-HD019011-13)
- Struggling before instruction improves conceptual learning, but scaffolding the struggle phase does not help and younger grades may need instruction first — [Sinha & Kapur](https://janfasen.nl/wp-content/uploads/2023/05/Sinha-and-Kapur-PS-I.pdf); [replication](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S0959475221000475)
- Unplugged CT activities are effective (g of about 0.6-1.0) — [K-9 meta](https://vufind-test.katalog.k.utb.cz/SummonRecord/FETCH-LOGICAL-c358t-e9bef564135f179e6073c16a124d28e9ac8dbbed29e2012ea2673cede1388c3e3)
- Children explore more when evidence is surprising or confounded — [PMC explore-exploit review](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11275514/)
- Augmentation modes for AI use are learning, iteration and validation — [Anthropic Jan 2026](https://www.anthropic.com/research/anthropic-economic-index-january-2026-report)
- Families are the front line of AI use, and AI risks displacing thinking — [Brookings 2026](https://webdocs.gmu.edu/wp-content/uploads/A-New-Direction-for-Students-in-an-AI-World.pdf)

### Inferences — Mechanic catalogue (with age variants and evidence anchor)

1. **Session ritual "Plan → Play → Show & Tell"** (Plan-Do-Review). *Anchor: metacognition A; PDR C.*
   - 2.5: tap one of 3 picture-choices ("today: animals / building / colours"). At the end, the parent asks "What did you do?" and the child points or says it. Under 60 seconds.
   - 5.5: pick a quest, predict "easy or tricky?" At the end, a 1-tap review: smiley/meh/hard face, plus "What tool helped?"
   - 8.5: set a goal ("finish 3 puzzles using a new strategy") and predict a score. Review compares prediction vs. result ("calibration star" for accurate self-prediction), plus a one-line "next time I'll…".

2. **Toolbox / Tool Belt.** *Anchor: Siegler, Hatano (theory-strong; direct RCT evidence absent).*
   - Each domain has a visible tool belt with icons. Kids pick a tool before solving. After solving they get "Was there another way?" A bonus comes from solving the *same* problem with 2 tools, and an "Efficiency badge" from finding the faster one.
   - 2.5: physical-world choices in mini-games (scoop vs. fork for water vs. pasta).
   - 5.5: 3 maths tools (count all / count on / draw it) and 3 reading tools.
   - 8.5: 6-8 tools including "work backwards", "smaller case", "make a table", "ask a person", "ask AI to explain (not to answer)". Kids can unlock "Invent a tool", which they name and add to their belt.
   - Interleaved "Mystery problem" rounds where the tool is not cued (variability).

3. **"Which helper?" tool-fit cards (AI-era).** *Anchor: Anthropic augmentation modes; Brookings.*
   - 6+: scenario cards ("You want to know how tall a giraffe is / spell a word / feel better after a fight / draw a dragon"). The child sorts each into My brain / A tool (ruler, book) / A person / AI / Experiment. Discussion follows, with no single right answer for some cards.
   - 8+: the "Try first, then check" rule. AI-assist unlocks only after a logged attempt, and the AI is framed as Explainer / Teammate / Checker, never Doer.

4. **Stretch Zone (opt-in challenge).** *Anchor: EEF challenge; PF; Hatano safe risk-taking.*
   - Every level offers Cozy / Stretch / Wild. Stretch and Wild give bigger rewards *for attempting*, not just succeeding ("Brave points"). Failure costs nothing.
   - 2.5: a "big puzzle" option (more pieces), always with a parent.
   - 5.5: chili-pepper difficulty icons.
   - 8.5: weekly "Boss problem" with a struggle-first window (5-10 min), then a hint ladder. Per Sinha & Kapur, don't over-scaffold the initial struggle, but give instruction after.

5. **Curiosity Quests: "Ask 5 questions" / question coins.** *Anchor: Engel, Tizard & Hughes (descriptive); constraint-seeking questions (Frontiers 2020).*
   - 2.5: "What's in the box?" Shake, peek, guess. The parent prompts "What do you wonder?"
   - 5.5: "Guess Who / 20 questions" training for constraint-seeking questions (big-category questions first). Wonder Wall: each question earns a coin, and *good* questions (testable ones) earn gold.
   - 8.5: Question Ladder: turn a "what" into a "why" and then a "what if", and pick one to investigate this week.

6. **Experiment Lab: "Test it!"** *Anchor: Gopnik/Schulz scientific thinking.*
   - 2.5: cause-effect sandbox (press, then see).
   - 5.5: blicket-style "which block makes the machine light up?" with confounded trials that invite re-testing. Predict, test, "Was I surprised?"
   - 8.5: fair-test builder (change one variable). "Surprise!" moments reward revising your belief ("Mind-changer medal" for intellectual humility).

7. **Invent It! weekly maker challenge (design-thinking cycle).** *Anchor: C (design thinking evidence thin); creativity, divergent thinking.*
   - Weekly real-world brief, done offline with photo upload: "Build a bridge for a toy car from paper", "Invent a gadget for Grandma".
   - Cycle: Empathize (who is it for?), Ideate (how many ideas? a divergent-thinking count), Build, Test, Improve.
   - 2.5: open-ended building with the parent, no brief.
   - 5.5: draw 3 ideas, then build 1.
   - 8.5: user interview of a family member, prototype, test, v2. Kid-entrepreneur extension: "Lemonade-stand-style" mini-business plan.

8. **Mistake Museum / "Mistake of the week".** *Anchor: Hatano safe risk-taking; intellectual humility; PF.*
   - Kids "donate" a mistake to their museum with the label "What I thought, what I learned". Family vote for "Most useful mistake". Parents add their own (behavioural modelling of "I don't know / I was wrong").
   - 2.5: parent narrates ("Oops, the tower fell. What can we try?").

9. **Thinking-routine mini-games (Project Zero lite).** *Anchor: C.*
   - See-Think-Wonder picture of the day (5.5+). Claim-Support-Question on "Is it true?" cards (8.5, also seeds AI-output skepticism). Think-Puzzle-Explore before a new topic. Keep each to 1-3 minutes.

10. **Debug Detective (computational thinking).** *Anchor: B+.*
    - Unplugged arrow-path robot puzzles with a deliberately wrong program: "find the bug". Loops for ages 6+. Decompose a chore into steps (8.5).

11. **Big Question Circle (P4C-lite, family mode).** *Anchor: B-.*
    - Weekly family dinner or Shabbat-table prompt from a picture book ("Is it ever OK to break a rule?"). Rules: give a reason, say "I agree/disagree because…". For an Israeli family, this can be culturally framed through *chavruta*-style paired argument (cultural fit; not evidence-graded).

12. **Systems Spinner.** *Anchor: C.*
    - 5.5: "If this happens, then what?" chains. 8.5: draw feedback loops (rabbits/foxes, screen time/sleep/mood). Name "reinforcing" vs. "balancing" loops.

13. **Passion Passport (explore, then commit).** *Anchor: Gopnik explore-exploit; Credé (interest consistency is the weak facet).*
    - Stamps for trying new domains. After about 4 weeks the child may pick a "Deep Dive season" (4-6 weeks) on one passion with a self-set goal and showcase. Switching is allowed without penalty.

14. **Calibration & Reflection Journal (8.5+, voice-first for younger).** *Anchor: metacognition A.*
    - Prompts: "What did I learn? What tool worked? What will I try next? How sure am I (1-5)?" Weekly "then vs. now" replay shows growth.

**Cross-cutting design principles**
- Praise *strategy and process* specifically, not traits ("smart"). Avoid mindset lectures.
- Every digital mechanic has an *offline transfer task* for family. This fits Brookings' families-as-front-line framing and keeps screen time bounded.
- Reward attempts, questions, belief revision and tool comparison, not just correct answers.
- **Age summary:**
  - 2.5: parent-mediated, physical play, choice between 2-3 pictures, narration of mistakes. No real "metacognition" beyond "what did you do?"
  - 5.5: named tools with icons, predict-then-check, constraint-seeking question games, unplugged CT, short see-think-wonder.
  - 8.5: full Pólya cycle, strategy comparison, struggle-first boss problems, fair tests, systems loops, AI-as-explainer/checker rules, calibration journal, maker projects with users.

### Gaps
- No RCTs were found testing these specific gamified mechanics (Stretch Zone, Toolbox, Mistake Museum) in apps for ages 2-10. They should be A/B tested inside the app (e.g., opt-in-challenge uptake, strategy-switch rates, question counts).
- The evidence on praise type (process vs. person praise, Mueller & Dweck 1998; Gunderson et al. 2013) was not retrieved this session. It comes from background knowledge and should be verified.
- The cultural adaptation (Hebrew, Israeli schooling context, chavruta) is my inference, not researched.
