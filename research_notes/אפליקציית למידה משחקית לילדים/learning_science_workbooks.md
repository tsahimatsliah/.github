## 1. Evidence and effect sizes for core learning techniques, and which ones transfer to young children

### Takeaway
Retrieval practice and spacing are the two most robust techniques. Both have direct evidence in children, including spacing at ages 2-3 and expanding retrieval at age 4. Interleaving, worked examples, the 85% rule, productive failure and "desirable difficulties" are mostly established in older students or adults. For ages 2-6 they need softening: more success, more guidance and less deliberate struggle. Feedback should be immediate and informative for young children. Mastery learning reliably works (about d≈0.5), but Bloom's "2 sigma" is an overstatement.

### Cited Findings
**Overall rankings**
- Dunlosky et al. (2013) rated **practice testing and distributed practice "high utility"** because they benefit learners of different ages and abilities across many tasks. **Elaborative interrogation, self-explanation and interleaved practice** were rated "moderate". **Summarization, highlighting, keyword mnemonic, imagery for text, and rereading** were rated "low". The authors caution that the techniques "are not a panacea" — [APS press release](https://www.psychologicalscience.org/news/releases/which-study-strategies-make-the-grade.html); [Dunlosky et al. 2013, PSPI, DOI](https://www.citedrive.com/en/discovery/improving-students-learning-with-effective-learning-techniques) [verified]. Most studies in this review involve students aged roughly 10 and older and college students, so its age generality is weaker for preschoolers (inference; see Gaps).

**Retrieval practice (testing effect)**
- Roediger & Karpicke (2006), college students: repeated testing beat repeated studying on a 1-week delayed test (about 61% vs 40% recall). Restudy looked better at 5 minutes — [Psychological Science, DOI 10.1111/j.1467-9280.2006.01693.x](https://doi.org/10.1111/j.1467-9280.2006.01693.x) [lit].
- Meta-analyses: Rowland (2014) g≈0.50 — [Psych Bulletin](https://doi.org/10.1037/a0037559) [lit]. Adesope et al. (2017) g≈0.61 — [RER](https://doi.org/10.3102/0034654316689306) [lit]. One secondary summary cites g = .70 across 272 effect sizes, so the reported figures conflict — [ResearchGate summary](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/362093173_The_science_of_effective_learning_with_spacing_and_retrieval_practice) [verified]. A reasonable reading is g≈0.5-0.7 in mostly adult or adolescent samples.
- Children: retrieval practice improved 4th-graders' learning — [Karpicke, Blunt & Smith 2016, Frontiers in Psychology](https://doi.org/10.3389/fpsyg.2016.00350) [lit]. Fazio & Marsh's review of "retrieval-based learning in children" concludes that testing benefits extend to young children, with caveats about the material and the support given — [Current Directions in Psych Science 2019](https://doi.org/10.1177/0963721418806673) [lit]. Expanding retrieval practice helped preschool children learn names — [Fritz, Morris, Nolan & Singleton 2007, QJEP](https://doi.org/10.1080/17470210600823595) [lit].
- Caution: the testing effect can shrink or vanish for **high element-interactivity** material such as complex problem solving for novices — [Leahy, Hanham & Sweller 2015, Ed Psych Review](https://doi.org/10.1007/s10648-015-9296-4) [lit].
- In a math-specific meta-analysis, **retrieval vs restudy was not statistically robust**, while **spaced practice showed a reliable small-to-medium benefit** — [Barton summary of 2025 meta-analysis](https://tipsforteachers.co.uk/research-a-meta-analytic-review-of-the-effectiveness-of-spacing-and-retrieval-practice-for-mathematics-learning/); [ResearchGate](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/394099339_A_Meta-analytic_Review_of_the_Effectiveness_of_Spacing_and_Retrieval_Practice_for_Mathematics_Learning) [verified].

**Spacing / distributed practice**
- Spaced retrieval beat massed retrieval with **g = 0.74**. **Expanding and uniform schedules did not differ significantly** — [Latimier, Peyre & Ramus 2021, Ed Psych Review (PDF)](http://www.lscp.net/persons/ramus/docs/EPR20.pdf) [verified].
- The optimal gap scales with the retention interval. Roughly, the best gap is about 10-20% of the retention interval for retention of about a week, falling to about 5-10% for retention of about a year (adults) — [Cepeda et al. 2008, Psych Science](https://doi.org/10.1111/j.1467-9280.2008.02209.x); [Cepeda et al. 2006 meta-analysis](https://doi.org/10.1037/0033-2909.132.3.354) [lit].
- **Children as young as 2-3:** 2-year-olds learned novel words better when exposures were spread across days than when massed — [Childers & Tomasello 2002, Dev Psych](https://doi.org/10.1037/0012-1649.38.6.967) [lit]. 3-year-olds generalized categories better with spaced presentations — [Vlach, Sandhofer & Kornell 2008, Cognition](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.cognition.2008.07.013) [lit]. First and second graders retained and generalized science concepts better with spaced lessons — [Vlach & Sandhofer 2012, Child Development](https://doi.org/10.1111/j.1467-8624.2012.01781.x) [lit].
- An elementary reading study found spaced **two-minute** sessions produced greater reading improvement — [Kang/ERIC review "Using Spacing to Enhance Diverse Forms of Learning"](https://files.eric.ed.gov/fulltext/ED536925.pdf) [verified].
- In 4-5-year-olds with developmental language disorder and matched controls (n=28), the spacing effect on word learning was small (b_std=0.23) and not significantly different from zero. Small sample — [PMC11087082](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC11087082/) [verified].

**Interleaving**
- Meta-analysis: overall g≈0.42. Interleaving is strong for visually similar categories (e.g., paintings), weaker for math, and negative for expository text and word lists — [Brunmair & Richter 2019, Psych Bulletin](https://doi.org/10.1037/bul0000209) [lit]. Interleaving and concept mapping were "less consistent" than spacing and testing — [ResearchGate review](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/362093173_The_science_of_effective_learning_with_spacing_and_retrieval_practice) [verified].
- 4th graders (about 9-10 years old) learning prism-geometry formulas: interleaved practice scored about 77% vs 38% for blocked practice at a 1-day test, even though performance during practice was lower — [Taylor & Rohrer 2010, Applied Cognitive Psych](https://doi.org/10.1002/acp.1598) [lit]. A classroom RCT in 7th-grade math showed a large interleaving benefit (d≈0.8) — [Rohrer et al. 2020, J Ed Psych](https://doi.org/10.1037/edu0000367) [lit].
- Most learners wrongly believe blocking works better. In Kornell & Bjork's study, most adults judged massed or blocked study as better even though interleaving won — [Kornell & Bjork 2008, Psych Science](https://doi.org/10.1111/j.1467-9280.2008.02127.x) [lit].

**Worked examples, cognitive load, expertise reversal**
- Worked-example effect: studying solved examples beats solving equivalent problems for novices — [Sweller & Cooper 1985](https://doi.org/10.1207/s1532690xci0201_3) [lit]. **Expertise reversal:** the advantage disappears or reverses as learners gain skill, so guidance must fade — [Kalyuga, Ayres, Chandler & Sweller 2003](https://doi.org/10.1207/S15326985EP3801_4) [lit]. Fading worked steps one at a time is effective — [Renkl & Atkinson 2003](https://doi.org/10.1207/S15326985EP3801_3) [lit].
- Working memory grows roughly linearly from age 4 through adolescence, so children hold markedly fewer elements than adults — [Gathercole et al. 2004, Dev Psych](https://doi.org/10.1037/0012-1649.40.2.177) [lit].
- I found no child-specific (under 8) worked-example effect size this session — [search result note](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/351889202_From_problem-solving_to_sensemaking_A_comparative_meta-analysis_of_preparatory_approaches_for_future_learning) [verified absence].

**Dual coding, multimedia, concreteness**
- Paivio's dual coding theory underlies Mayer's multimedia principle (words plus pictures beat words alone). The evidence base is mostly college students — [Mayer, Multimedia Learning](https://doi.org/10.1017/CBO9780511811678) [lit].
- "Learning styles" matching (visual vs auditory learner) is unsupported — [Pashler et al. 2008, PSPI](https://doi.org/10.1111/j.1539-6053.2009.01038.x) [lit].
- **Concreteness fading** (concrete, then pictorial, then abstract) outperforms concrete-only or abstract-only instruction for transfer — [Fyfe, McNeil, Son & Goldstone 2014, Ed Psych Review](https://doi.org/10.1007/s10648-014-9249-3) [lit].
- **Extraneous perceptual detail hurts young children.** Children learned math concepts less well from perceptually rich, decorative representations than from simple ones — [Kaminski & Sloutsky 2013, J Ed Psych](https://doi.org/10.1037/a0032170) [lit]. Kindergartners in a heavily decorated classroom were more off-task and learned less than in a sparse room — [Fisher, Godwin & Seltman 2014, Psych Science](https://doi.org/10.1177/0956797614533801) [lit].

**Feedback; immediate vs delayed; errorless vs errorful**
- Feedback interventions average d≈0.41, but about one-third of them *decreased* performance. Feedback aimed at the self or ego ("you're smart") rather than the task does worst — [Kluger & DeNisi 1996, Psych Bulletin](https://doi.org/10.1037/0033-2909.119.2.254) [lit]. Hattie & Timperley give a higher average (d≈0.79) and stress task- and process-level feedback — [RER 2007](https://doi.org/10.3102/003465430298487) [lit]. A later re-meta-analysis gives d≈0.48 — [Wisniewski, Zierer & Hattie 2020, Frontiers](https://doi.org/10.3389/fpsyg.2019.03087) [lit].
- Timing: in applied classroom studies immediate feedback was generally better. In lab list-learning studies delayed feedback was often better — [Kulik & Kulik 1988, RER](https://doi.org/10.3102/00346543058001079) [lit]. Adult studies showing delayed-feedback advantages may not generalize to young children (inference).
- Errors followed by corrective feedback can enhance learning (the error-generation and hypercorrection literature, mostly adults) — [Metcalfe 2017, Annual Review of Psychology](https://doi.org/10.1146/annurev-psych-010416-044022) [lit].
- **Productive failure:** Sinha & Kapur's (2021) meta-analysis covers 166 comparisons from 53 studies and finds that problem solving before instruction beats instruction first with **g=0.36 [0.20, 0.51]**. Effects are larger when designs follow productive-failure principles more closely — [Sinha & Kapur 2021, RER (PDF)](https://janfasen.nl/wp-content/uploads/2023/05/Sinha-and-Kapur-PS-I.pdf) [verified]. A secondary source says **for grades 2-5 effects favored instruction-first** (unverified against the paper) — [mindomax summary](https://www.mindomax.com/productive-failure) [verified as claim only]. The aggregate effect across three meta-analyses of "productive failure (errors)" is 0.39 — [Visible Learning MetaX](https://www.visiblelearningmetax.com/influences/view/productive_failure_(errors)) [verified]. The evidence base is mostly middle school, high school and university students (inference from the paper's population descriptions).

**Mastery learning and Bloom's 2 sigma**
- Bloom (1984) reported that 1:1 tutoring with mastery methods produced about 2 SD gains (the tutored student above 98% of controls) and that group mastery learning produced about 1 SD — [Wikipedia summary](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bloom's_2_sigma_problem); [Bloom 1984, Educational Researcher](https://doi.org/10.3102/0013189X013006004) [verified].
- Critiques: the 2-sigma figure came from two dissertations with three-week units and custom tests. Much of the effect may come from holding tutees to a higher mastery standard. Slavin found effects near zero in stricter studies, and small studies show larger effects (about .38 for n<100 vs about .11 for n>2000) — [Nintil systematic review](https://nintil.com/bloom-sigma/) [verified]. VanLehn (2011) estimated human tutoring at about d=0.79 and found no 2-sigma replication — [Education Next](https://www.educationnext.org/two-sigma-tutoring-separating-science-fiction-from-science-fact/) [verified]; [VanLehn 2011](https://doi.org/10.1080/00461520.2011.611369) [lit].
- Mastery learning meta-analysis: average effect about 0.52 — [Kulik, Kulik & Bangert-Drowns 1990, RER](https://doi.org/10.3102/00346543060002265) [lit]. A commonly cited average for mastery learning is 0.59 — [Wikipedia: Mastery learning](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mastery_learning) [verified].

**Deliberate practice**
- Deliberate practice explained about 26% of performance variance in games, 21% in music, 18% in sports, **4% in education** and under 1% in professions — [Macnamara, Hambrick & Oswald 2014, Psych Science](https://doi.org/10.1177/0956797614535810) [lit]. Its structure (targeted weaknesses, immediate feedback, repetition) is useful, but it is not a strong predictor in education, and it was developed for older, motivated learners (inference).

**The "85% rule" (Wilson et al. 2019)**
- For gradient-descent learners on binary classification tasks with Gaussian noise, the optimal training error rate is **≈15.87%** (about 85% accuracy). The authors tested it in perceptrons, two-layer networks and a model of animal perceptual learning, and argued it can make learning exponentially faster than training at fixed difficulty — [Nature Communications 10:4646](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41467-019-12552-4) [lit; the page was blocked this session, but the paper is listed in the verified bibliography of this research note: [kilowatto/math-challenge research note](https://github.com/kilowatto/math-challenge/blob/main/docs/research/2026-07-31-mc-13-its-knowledge-tracing-elo.md)].
- The result is theoretical. It has not been tested on children's curricular learning, and it ignores motivation (inference based on the model's assumptions).
- Converging but independent classroom guidance: Rosenshine reports an optimal success rate during guided practice of "about 80 percent" — [Rosenshine 2012, American Educator](https://www.aft.org/sites/default/files/periodicals/Rosenshine.pdf) [lit]. Math Garden targets **75%** by default — [Klinkenberg et al. 2011 via ERIC](https://eric.ed.gov/?id=EJ925823) [verified].

**Desirable difficulties (Bjork)**
- Conditions that slow apparent performance (spacing, interleaving, testing, variation) can enhance long-term retention. They are "desirable" only if the learner can respond to them successfully — [Bjork & Bjork 2011 (PDF)](https://bjorklab.psych.ucla.edu/wp-content/uploads/sites/13/2016/04/EBjork_RBjork_2011.pdf) [lit].

### Inferences
- **Adult-only or mostly adult evidence:** delayed-feedback advantages, productive failure, the 85% rule (no humans tested), the multimedia principle's large effects, and most interleaving effects (child evidence starts around age 9). **Child-supported evidence:** spacing (from age 2), retrieval with support (from age 4), guided play (ages 1-8), concreteness fading, minimal-decoration layouts, and immediate task-focused feedback.
- For the 2.5-year-old, "retrieval" should be recognition-based ("Where is the dog?" with 2-3 choices) rather than free recall, and spacing across days matters more than any within-session algorithm.
- For the 8.5-year-old, the adult techniques start to apply: interleaved mixed review, a few worked examples followed by faded problems, and modest productive struggle, with a hint available after one or two failures.

### Gaps
- No pooled effect size exists for retrieval, spacing or interleaving specifically in ages 2-6. The child evidence comes from individual studies.
- No child-specific meta-analysis of immediate vs delayed feedback was found.
- The Kaminski & Sloutsky figures and the Fisher et al. classroom percentages were not re-fetched this session.

## 2. ZPD, scaffolding and fading, multi-sensory and embodied learning, play-based learning, guided vs free play

### Takeaway
Guided play is the evidence-backed sweet spot for ages 2-8. In it, an adult or system sets a learning goal and the child keeps agency and choice. It beats free play for specific content such as shapes, spatial vocabulary and early math, and matches or beats direct instruction for some outcomes. Scaffolding should be contingent: more help after an error, less after success. Embodied, linear and spatial representations, such as a number line traversed by moving, have strong child evidence.

### Cited Findings
- Scaffolding was defined as an adult "controlling those elements of the task that are initially beyond the learner's capacity," including recruitment, reducing degrees of freedom, direction maintenance, marking critical features, frustration control and demonstration — [Wood, Bruner & Ross 1976, JCPP](https://doi.org/10.1111/j.1469-7610.1976.tb00381.x) [lit].
- Skene et al. (2022) meta-analysis of guided play: 39 studies (17 meta-analysed), 3,893 children aged about 1-8. Results were mixed. Guided play showed advantages for math and spatial outcomes. Spatial/maths vocabulary vs free play: ES 0.93, p=.03, but only 3 studies and I²≈81%. Early literacy vs direct instruction: ES 0.28, not significant. Definitions of guided play varied — [Wiley/Child Development](https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/10.1111/cdev.13730); [PDF](https://wrap2fasd.org/wp-content/uploads/2025/01/Child-Development-2022-Skene-Can-guidance-during-play-enhance-children-s-learning-and-development-in-educational.pdf) [verified].
- Guided play (an adult-scaffolded discovery game: "detectives" finding the "secret" of shapes) beat both didactic instruction and free play for 4-5-year-olds' learning of geometric shapes — [Fisher, Hirsh-Pasek, Newcombe & Golinkoff 2013, Child Development](https://doi.org/10.1111/cdev.12091) [lit]. Guided play is defined as child-directed, adult-scaffolded play toward a learning goal — [Weisberg, Hirsh-Pasek, Golinkoff et al. 2016, Current Directions](https://doi.org/10.1177/0963721416645512) [lit].
- Play is best understood as a spectrum: free play, guided play, games, and playful instruction — [Zosh et al. 2018, Frontiers in Psychology](https://doi.org/10.3389/fpsyg.2018.01124) [lit]. The LEGO Foundation and Zosh et al. "Learning through Play" framework names five characteristics: **joyful, actively engaging, meaningful, iterative, socially interactive** (same source family; label wording from the user's brief matches this framework) [lit].
- **Four Pillars for educational apps:** active ("minds-on," not just tapping), engaged (not distracted by bells and whistles), meaningful (connected to the child's life), and socially interactive, all within a defined learning goal — [Hirsh-Pasek et al. 2015, PSPI (PDF)](https://kathyhirshpasek.com/wp-content/uploads/sites/9/2019/06/HirshPasek_ScienceofLearningApps.pdf); [Semantic Scholar](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/Putting-Education-in-%E2%80%9CEducational%E2%80%9D-Apps-Hirsh-Pasek-Zosh/94b20e2b1b084100c811a1c82b7d33517e1b0fcb) [verified]. A 2021 app-store content analysis operationalized the pillars into a coding scheme and found most top "educational" apps scored low — [Meyer et al. 2021 (PDF)](https://kathyhirshpasek.com/wp-content/uploads/sites/9/2021/03/How-educational-are-educational-apps-for-young-children-App-store-content-analysis-using-the-Four-Pillars-of-Learning-framework.pdf) [verified existence; the "most scored low" result is lit]. In 2025 Hirsh-Pasek restated that children learn best when "active (minds on)... engaged not distracted... meaningful... socially interactive" — [Brookings 2025](https://www.brookings.edu/articles/looking-for-an-educational-life-raft-apps-may-not-be-the-answer/) [verified].
- **Embodied and spatial number learning:** low-income preschoolers who played a *linear* 1-10 number board game (counting aloud while moving a token) for about four 15-20 minute sessions over 2 weeks improved in number-line estimation, magnitude comparison, counting and number identification. A *circular* board, or color version, did not help — [Ramani & Siegler 2008, Child Development](https://doi.org/10.1111/j.1467-8624.2007.01131.x) [lit].
- Concreteness fading (manipulatives, then pictures, then symbols) supports transfer — [Fyfe et al. 2014](https://doi.org/10.1007/s10648-014-9249-3) [lit]. This is the same logic as Singapore Math's Concrete-Pictorial-Abstract sequence (inference).
- Multisensory presentation is distinct from "learning styles". Matching instruction to a preferred modality has no support — [Pashler et al. 2008](https://doi.org/10.1111/j.1539-6053.2009.01038.x) [lit].

### Inferences
- ZPD in software means two things: (a) item selection at the edge of competence (Section 3) and (b) contingent hints. Use the rule "fail, then add a hint level; succeed, then remove one", which mirrors Wood et al.'s contingent-tutoring logic.
- For the 2.5-year-old, the product should be closer to guided play with a parent: open exploration with occasional gentle prompts ("Can you find the red one?"), not drill.
- For the 5.5-year-old, use game-like guided play with embedded goals (linear board-game mechanics for number, letter-sound treasure hunts).
- For the 8.5-year-old, use playful practice and quests, with more explicit goals and self-chosen challenge.

### Gaps
- No meta-analytic evidence comparing digital guided play with physical guided play was found.
- The exact LEGO Foundation report URL was not verified this session.

## 3. Adaptive learning and knowledge-tracing approaches for a small app (BKT, Elo/Math Garden, Leitner, SM-2, FSRS, Duolingo HLR, mastery thresholds)

### Takeaway
For a small family app, the most practical engine is **Elo-style ability and difficulty estimation (as in Math Garden) for choosing items at a target success rate**, plus a **lightweight spaced-repetition scheduler** (Leitner boxes or a simplified half-life/FSRS-style model) for review of mastered items. A per-skill mastery flag can come from BKT (P(known)≥0.95) or from a simple streak or accuracy rule. Math Garden shows that the success-rate target interacts with motivation. Easier settings (more success) led children to practice more.

### Cited Findings
**Math Garden / Rekentuin (Klinkenberg, Straatemeier & van der Maas 2011)**
- A web-based **computer adaptive practice (CAP)** and monitoring system for Dutch primary arithmetic. It uses an adapted **Elo rating system** that updates child ability and item difficulty "on the fly" after every answer, with no pre-calibration — [Computers & Education 57(2):1813-1824; ERIC abstract](https://eric.ed.gov/?id=EJ925823); [ACM DL](https://dl.acm.org/doi/abs/10.1016/J.COMPEDU.2011.02.003) [verified].
- The scoring rule accounts for **both accuracy and response time**. Items are sampled so the **mean success probability is about 0.75**, "challenging yet not too difficult" — [ScienceDirect abstract](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S0360131511000418) [verified via search snippet].
- **High Speed High Stakes (HSHS) scoring rule:** score = a·(d − RT)·(2·acc − 1), where d is the item time limit, RT the response time and acc ∈ {0,1}. A fast correct answer earns many points and a fast wrong answer loses many, which discourages guessing. Under this rule the implied model is a 2PL-type IRT model with the time limit acting as discrimination — [Maris & van der Maas 2012, Psychometrika](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s11336-012-9288-y) as summarized in the [kilowatto research note](https://github.com/kilowatto/math-challenge/blob/main/docs/research/2026-07-31-mc-13-its-knowledge-tracing-elo.md) [verified secondary; formula flagged as secondary]. The HSHS rule shows "strong validity and reliability" across math, statistics and chess — [Klinkenberg, HSHS (academia.edu)](https://www.academia.edu/61121229/High_Speed_High_Stakes_Scoring_Rule) [verified].
- Criterion validity of Math Garden ratings against national CITO tests: r=.78-.84 — [Klinkenberg 2011, cited in a T&F paper](https://www.tandfonline.com/doi/pdf/10.1080/01443410.2016.1150421?needAccess=true) [verified].
- Later change: the RT-dependent penalty for wrong answers was replaced by a **fixed penalty of 2 coins**, keeping the same score for correct answers — [Psychometrics of an Elo-based large-scale online learning system, 2025](https://sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S2666920X25000165?via=ihub%3D) [verified via search snippet].
- Elo update logic (general form used in Math Garden): θ_child ← θ_child + K·(S − E[S]); β_item ← β_item − K·(S − E[S]), where E[S] is the expected score given θ − β — [Klinkenberg et al. 2011](https://eric.ed.gov/?id=EJ925823) [lit for the equation form]. A practical extension is an **uncertainty-dependent K** (large for new children and items, decaying with the number of attempts), e.g. U(n)=α/(1+β·n) — [Pelánek 2016, "Applications of the Elo rating system in adaptive educational systems" (PDF)](https://www.fi.muni.cz/~xpelanek/publications/CAE-elo.pdf) [lit]. The kilowatto note's suggestion of K≈0.5-1.0 for the first 10-20 attempts decaying to about 0.05-0.1 is that note's own heuristic, not Math Garden's value — [kilowatto note](https://github.com/kilowatto/math-challenge/blob/main/docs/research/2026-07-31-mc-13-its-knowledge-tracing-elo.md) [verified]. A tracking variant with explicit uncertainty was published by the Math Garden team — [Hofman et al. 2020, "Tracking with (un)certainty," J Intelligence](https://doi.org/10.3390/jintelligence8010010) [lit].
- **Difficulty vs motivation:** Jansen et al. (2013) randomized Math Garden children to easy (P(success)≈.90), medium (≈.75) or hard (≈.60) conditions for six weeks. Children in the easier condition **practiced more problems**, and the amount practiced related to performance gains. Math-anxiety effects were limited — [Learning and Individual Differences, "The influence of experiencing success in math on math anxiety, perceived math competence, and math performance"](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S1041608012001951) [lit for the condition values and results; the paper's existence and six-week design were verified via [T&F paper snippet](https://www.tandfonline.com/doi/pdf/10.1080/01443410.2016.1150421?needAccess=true)].
- Math Garden interface features (as described in the team's papers): a per-item countdown (commonly 20 s) shown as disappearing coins; a **question-mark ("I don't know") button** that avoids penalty and guessing; coins earned are spent on rewards; each game is a **plant in the child's garden that grows with practice and wilts when not practiced**, a built-in nudge toward spaced return — [Klinkenberg et al. 2011](https://eric.ed.gov/?id=EJ925823) [lit; not re-verified this session, see Gaps].
- Multidimensional Elo extensions for tracking multiple skills are an active research area (EDM 2025) — [EDM 2025 proceedings](https://educationaldatamining.org/EDM2025/proceedings/2025.EDM.long-papers.99/index.html) [verified existence].

**Bayesian Knowledge Tracing**
- BKT has four parameters per skill: P(L0) prior knowledge, P(T) learning rate, P(G) guess and P(S) slip. Illustrative values: P(L0)=0.36, P(T)=0.1, P(G)=0.3, P(S)=0.05. The model is well-behaved when P(G)+P(S)<1 — [van de Sande 2013, JEDM (PDF)](https://files.eric.ed.gov/fulltext/EJ1115329.pdf) via [kilowatto note](https://github.com/kilowatto/math-challenge/blob/main/docs/research/2026-07-31-mc-13-its-knowledge-tracing-elo.md) [verified]. A skill is conventionally treated as "mastered" when P(L)≥0.95 — [Corbett & Anderson 1995, UMUAI](https://doi.org/10.1007/BF01099821) [lit].
- Deep Knowledge Tracing reported AUC 0.85 vs 0.68 for BKT on Khan data. Later work showed that a well-tuned BKT or simple models close much of the gap — [Piech et al. 2015](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1506.05908); [Khajah, Lindsey & Mozer 2016](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1604.02416), as listed in the [kilowatto note](https://github.com/kilowatto/math-challenge/blob/main/docs/research/2026-07-31-mc-13-its-knowledge-tracing-elo.md) [verified]. For three users, deep models are unnecessary (inference).

**Spaced-repetition schedulers**
- **Leitner system:** cards move up a box on success and back to box 1 on failure. Higher boxes are reviewed less often (e.g., box 1 daily, box 2 every 2-3 days, box 3 weekly) — [Leitner system, Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Leitner_system) [lit].
- **SM-2 (Wozniak):** I(1)=1 day, I(2)=6 days, I(n)=I(n−1)·EF. EF starts at 2.5 with a minimum of 1.3 and is updated by EF' = EF + (0.1 − (5−q)(0.08 + (5−q)·0.02)) for quality q∈0-5. If q<3, repetitions restart — [SuperMemo SM-2 description](https://super-memory.com/english/ol/sm2.htm) [lit].
- **FSRS-6:** a DSR model with Difficulty D∈[1,10], Stability S (the interval at which R=90%) and Retrievability R. Forgetting curve R(t,S) = (1 + factor·t/S)^(−w20), where factor = 0.9^(−1/w20) − 1. There are **21 parameters**, with defaults starting 0.212, 1.2931, 2.3065, 8.2956. Grades are 1 again, 2 hard, 3 good, 4 easy. Same-day review stability S' = S·e^(w17·(G−3+w18))·S^(−w19). The interval for a target retention r is t = S/factor·(r^(−1/w20) − 1) (derived) — [FSRS wiki, The Algorithm](https://github.com/open-spaced-repetition/awesome-fsrs/wiki/The-Algorithm) [verified]. The commonly used default desired retention is 0.9 (Anki/FSRS default) [lit; not stated on the fetched page].
- **Duolingo Half-Life Regression:** p = 2^(−Δ/h), with half-life h = 2^(Θ·x) learned from features (counts of past correct and incorrect answers, lexeme features). It reduced recall-prediction error by **45%+ vs baselines** (Leitner, Pimsleur, logistic regression) and improved **daily student engagement by 12%** in an operational A/B test — [Settles & Meeder 2016, ACL (PDF)](https://research.duolingo.com/papers/settles.acl16.pdf); [Semantic Scholar](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/A-Trainable-Spaced-Repetition-Model-for-Language-Settles-Meeder/cb836d2b8e126dc31ded5e674d73021604dcc6e0); [code](https://github.com/duolingo/halflife-regression) [verified]. A "16% vs SM-2" figure seen on Medium is unverified — [Medium](https://medium.com/@sagarkumarharwani19/spaced-repetition-technique-72fadf140f23) [verified as claim only].
- Expanding vs uniform spacing schedules did not differ significantly in meta-analysis — [Latimier et al. 2021](http://www.lscp.net/persons/ramus/docs/EPR20.pdf) [verified]. So scheduler sophistication matters less than *having* spaced review.

### Inferences
- Recommended architecture (small app, three kids):
  1. **Skill graph:** each skill (e.g., "count to 5", "letter ב sound", "add within 10", "multiply by 3") has prerequisites.
  2. **Elo per child per skill-domain, plus per-item difficulty.** Initialize item difficulty by hand (designer-ordered, Kumon-style steps). Use uncertainty-decaying K. Pick the next item whose predicted P(correct) is near the target band.
  3. **Mastery flag** per skill: BKT P(L)≥0.95, or the simpler rule "≥4 of last 5 correct over ≥2 separate days" (the two-day requirement builds spacing into mastery; a design choice, not sourced).
  4. **Review scheduler** for mastered skills: Leitner-like boxes or HLR/FSRS-lite, with intervals of 1, 3, 7, 14, 30 days and a target recall of about 0.85-0.9. A lapse demotes the skill and re-opens practice.
- Treat response time only as a *soft* signal for under-6s. Motor and tapping speed confounds RT at that age, so HSHS time pressure is inappropriate for the 2.5- and 5.5-year-olds and optional for the 8.5-year-old (inference from the HSHS design rationale).

### Gaps
- Math Garden's exact K values, the 20-second limit and the garden and wilting mechanic were not re-verified this session (publisher and university hosts were blocked). Exact numbers from Jansen et al. 2013 were also not re-fetched.
- No published adaptive engine specifically for ages 2-4 was found. Most adaptive-learning systems start at about age 4-6.
- FSRS's 0.9 default desired retention comes from general knowledge of FSRS and Anki and was not stated on the fetched page.

## 4. Attention span and session length by age; session frequency

### Takeaway
Hard evidence on "attention span in minutes" is weak. Most widely circulated figures come from blogs. The best-supported concrete anchors are the AAP guidance (ages 2-5: about 1 hour/day total of high-quality media, co-viewed) and effective intervention dosages: about 15-20 minute sessions for preschool board games, and brief two-minute spaced sessions for reading. Design for short, frequent, spaced sessions that end on success.

### Cited Findings
- AAP: under 18-24 months, avoid screens except video chat. For ages 2-5, limit to **about 1 hour/day of high-quality programming, co-viewed with a parent** — [AAP "Media and Young Minds" 2016, Pediatrics](https://doi.org/10.1542/peds.2016-2591) [lit]; [AAP screen-time Q&A](https://www.aap.org/en/patient-care/media-and-children/center-of-excellence-on-social-media-and-youth-mental-health/qa-portal/qa-portal-library/qa-portal-library-questions/screen-time-for-infants/); [APA Monitor 2020](https://www.apa.org/monitor/2020/04/cover-kids-screens) [verified]. Several 2026 blogs mention an "AAP 2026 update" (e.g., the "5 Cs"), but I could not verify this against AAP primary text — [family-checklist.com](https://family-checklist.com/blog/aap-screen-time-guidelines-2026.html) [verified as claim only].
- Attention rules of thumb (non-peer-reviewed): "about 2-5 minutes per year of age". Other estimates: about 5-10 minutes at 3-4, 10-15 at 5-6, 15-25 at 7-8, and 25-40 at 9-12. Sources disagree — [Neeuro blog](https://www.neeuro.com/blog/attention-span-by-age); [edfirefly](https://edfirefly.com/attention-span-in-children/) [verified; low quality].
- Peer-reviewed research shows sustained attention develops across childhood, with attention components differentiating around age 9 — [Latent factors in attention emerge from 9 years, PMC5633677](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC5633677/) [verified existence]. Pupillometry studies of young children's sustained attention exist — [PMC9680391](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC9680391/) [verified existence].
- Effective dosages in child intervention studies: four 15-20 minute board-game sessions over 2 weeks improved preschoolers' number knowledge — [Ramani & Siegler 2008](https://doi.org/10.1111/j.1467-8624.2007.01131.x) [lit]. Spaced two-minute reading sessions outperformed massed practice — [ERIC ED536925](https://files.eric.ed.gov/fulltext/ED536925.pdf) [verified].
- A home math app used with parents (Bedtime Math) for short nightly sessions produced meaningful first-grade math gains, especially for children of math-anxious parents — [Berkowitz et al. 2015, Science](https://doi.org/10.1126/science.aac7427) [lit].

### Inferences
Suggested defaults (design choices grounded in the anchors above, not empirically tested norms):
- **Age 2-3 (2.5-year-old):** 3-5 minute micro-sessions, with a parent present. At most about 2-3 sessions/day, within the AAP 1-hour total. Each activity lasts 30-90 s. Daily or near-daily use matters more than session length (spacing evidence from Childers & Tomasello).
- **Age 5-6 (5.5-year-old):** 8-12 minute sessions (about 15 min max), 1-2/day, 5-6 days/week, made up of 4-6 activities of 1-3 minutes each.
- **Age 8-9 (8.5-year-old):** 15-20 minute sessions (cap at about 25 min), 1/day, 5 days/week, with roughly 10-15 items of new practice and 5-10 of mixed review.
- Always offer an end point ("you finished today's garden!") rather than infinite play. End on a success. Detect fatigue (rising RT, accuracy dropping 2+ items below the target band, rapid random taps) and suggest a break.

### Gaps
- No peer-reviewed normative table of "minutes of sustained attention per age" for screen-based learning tasks was found.
- No RCT comparing app session lengths for ages 2-10 was found.

## 5. Workbook design: what makes top workbooks effective

### Takeaway
The best workbooks share a recognizable pattern. They use **tiny graded steps** (Kumon) and **start below the child's level for early success**. They include a **worked example or model at the top of the page**. Practice moves **concrete → pictorial → abstract** (Singapore Math, Montessori). Layouts are **clean, uncluttered and one concept per page**. Mixed review is built in, along with **self-checking** (Montessori "control of error", answer keys) and **visible progress** (stickers, charts). Most of this comes from publishers' own claims and design traditions, while the underlying principles (small steps, concreteness fading, low clutter, spaced mixed review) have independent research support.

### Cited Findings
- **Kumon** (company's own description): worksheets increase in difficulty in **small increments**, each "one small step more advanced than the lesson before." Students **start on content they can do easily, aiming for full marks** to build achievement and motivation, and work at a "just right" level. Worksheets include **examples and hints so students can solve problems independently**. Each worksheet has a **standard completion time**. An assignment is complete only when **every problem is correct**. Worksheets are revised using student reactions — [Kumon: Five Features of Small-Step Worksheets](https://www.kumon.com/ca-en/about-kumon/kumon-method/small-steps/five-features); [Kumon Group: Small-Step Worksheets](https://www.kumongroup.com/eng/about-kumon/method/small-steps/); [Kumon Parents Guide (PDF)](https://www.kumon.com/assets/us-en/pdfs/KUMON_PARENTS_GUIDE.pdf) [verified; marketing material, no independent evaluation found].
- **Self-learning** emphasis: Kumon describes students solving from worksheet examples rather than teacher lectures — [Kumon Self-Learning](https://www.kumon.com/about-kumon/kumon-method/self-learning) [verified].
- **Japanese drill tradition:** "百ます計算" (hyaku-masu keisan, the 100-cell grid) is timed arithmetic practice popularized by Kageyama Hideo. Children fill a 10×10 grid of operations, tracking their time to show improvement — [Japanese Wikipedia: 百ます計算](https://ja.wikipedia.org/wiki/%E7%99%BE%E3%81%BE%E3%81%99%E8%A8%88%E7%AE%97) [lit].
- **Montessori:** materials are self-correcting ("control of error"), concrete and sequenced. Montessori preschool showed elevated and more equal outcomes in a lottery-based longitudinal study — [Lillard et al. 2017, Frontiers in Psychology](https://doi.org/10.3389/fpsyg.2017.01783) [lit].
- **Singapore Math:** Concrete-Pictorial-Abstract progression and bar models. The research basis is concreteness fading — [Fyfe et al. 2014](https://doi.org/10.1007/s10648-014-9249-3) [lit].
- **Visual layout:** extraneous decoration reduces young children's learning — [Fisher et al. 2014](https://doi.org/10.1177/0956797614533801); [Kaminski & Sloutsky 2013](https://doi.org/10.1037/a0032170) [lit].
- **Mixed review:** spiral and interleaved review pages (as in Saxon-style or Japanese drill review) align with spacing and interleaving evidence — [Rohrer et al. 2020](https://doi.org/10.1037/edu0000367); [Latimier et al. 2021](http://www.lscp.net/persons/ramus/docs/EPR20.pdf) [lit/verified].
- **Stickers and rewards:** expected, tangible, task-contingent rewards undermine intrinsic motivation (meta-analytic d≈−0.34 for expected tangible rewards). Verbal, informational praise enhances it — [Deci, Koestner & Ryan 1999, Psych Bulletin](https://doi.org/10.1037/0033-2909.125.6.627) [lit]. Praising effort or process rather than intelligence leads children to choose challenges and persist after failure — [Mueller & Dweck 1998, JPSP](https://doi.org/10.1037/0022-3514.75.1.33) [lit].

### Inferences
Transferable "page" rules for the app:
- One concept per screen. A model or worked example appears at the top for new skills, then fades.
- Ten or fewer items per "page". The first two are very easy (warm-up, Kumon full-marks start). The last is a "victory" item.
- Every third or fourth page is a mixed review page.
- Self-checking: the child can tap "check", and items are designed so the error is visible (Montessori control of error).
- Stickers mark *progress and effort* (completion, comeback after a mistake, practice streak) rather than serving as a bribe for each answer. Unexpected surprise rewards are less undermining than promised ones (Deci et al.).

### Gaps
- No independent evaluation of Kumon, Gakken, Spectrum, Brain Quest or Evan-Moor workbooks was found. Their principles are described from their own materials or the design tradition.
- **Israeli workbooks (Sifrei Yesod, Keshet, Matmatikon, etc.):** no reliable sources were found this session. Their design principles and Ministry of Education approval status still need research, ideally in Hebrew.
- Gakken-specific design principles were not found (a Japanese-language search is needed).

## 6. Metacognition, self-regulation, parental involvement, and motivation

### Takeaway
Metacognition and self-regulation interventions have large average effects (EEF: +8 months as of 2025), including in early years and primary. For young children these must be scaffolded and externalized: simple plan-do-review, picture-based self-rating, and visible goals. Parental involvement has mixed general evidence, but specific co-use designs work, such as shared math activities and dialogic interactions. Co-use is also what the AAP recommends for ages 2-5.

### Cited Findings
- **EEF Metacognition and Self-regulation:** a May 2025 update with 107 new studies raised the average impact to **+8 months** (previously +7). High effects are seen in early years, primary and secondary — [EEF Toolkit](https://educationendowmentfoundation.org.uk/education-evidence/teaching-learning-toolkit/metacognition-and-self-regulation); [RISE magazine on the update](https://www.riseedumag.com/eef-teaching-and-learning-toolkit-updated/) [verified]. The EEF guidance report recommends explicit planning, monitoring and evaluation cycles — [EEF Guidance Report (PDF)](https://d2tic4wvo1iusb.cloudfront.net/production/eef-guidance-reports/metacognition/metacognition-and-self-regulated-learning_guidance-report.v.2.4.0.pdf) [verified existence].
- EEF early years self-regulation and executive function evidence store (15 studies) — [EEF Early Years](https://educationendowmentfoundation.org.uk/early-years/evidence-store/self-regulation-and-executive-function) [verified].
- **Parental engagement:** EEF says the evidence is "mixed and much less conclusive, particularly for disadvantaged families." The 2014 toolkit listed +3 months. A current months figure (often cited as +4) was **not verified** — [Sutton Trust-EEF 2014 (PDF)](https://thehub-beta.walthamforest.gov.uk/sites/default/files/2019-07/sutton_trust_toolkit.pdf); [EEF 2018 toolkit](https://dera.ioe.ac.uk/id/eprint/33734/1/EEF-Teaching-Learning-Toolkit-October-2018.pdf) [verified].
- Parent-child co-use of a math app (Bedtime Math) improved children's math achievement over a school year, particularly when parents were math-anxious — [Berkowitz et al. 2015, Science](https://doi.org/10.1126/science.aac7427) [lit].
- Executive-function-focused preschool curriculum (Tools of the Mind: play plans, self-talk, buddy reading) improved EF in an RCT — [Diamond, Barnett, Thomas & Munro 2007, Science](https://doi.org/10.1126/science.1151148) [lit].
- AAP recommends co-viewing and co-use for ages 2-5 — [AAP 2016](https://doi.org/10.1542/peds.2016-2591) [lit].
- Socially interactive learning is one of the four pillars, and social contingency boosts young children's learning from media — [Hirsh-Pasek et al. 2015](https://kathyhirshpasek.com/wp-content/uploads/sites/9/2019/06/HirshPasek_ScienceofLearningApps.pdf) [verified].
- Feedback aimed at the person rather than the task harms performance — [Kluger & DeNisi 1996](https://doi.org/10.1037/0033-2909.119.2.254) [lit]. Effort and process praise supports persistence — [Mueller & Dweck 1998](https://doi.org/10.1037/0022-3514.75.1.33) [lit].
- In the HLR study, better scheduling raised engagement by 12%. Good adaptivity is itself motivating — [Settles & Meeder 2016](https://research.duolingo.com/papers/settles.acl16.pdf) [verified].
- More success in Math Garden led children to practice more — [Jansen et al. 2013](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S1041608012001951) [lit].

### Inferences
- For the 2.5-year-old, metacognition means turn-taking and naming feelings ("That was tricky! Let's try again"). The parent is the scaffold.
- For the 5.5-year-old, use a picture-based plan-do-review: "What will you play today?" (choose 1 of 3 icons), then a self-rating at the end (easy / just right / tricky, shown as three faces), then compare it with the actual performance shown by the system.
- For the 8.5-year-old, use goal setting ("today: 3 garden plants"), prediction before a challenge ("How many will you get right?"), a weekly reflection, and a choice of challenge level (as in Math Garden's easy/medium/hard).
- Parent dashboard: a short daily summary with **one suggested offline conversation or activity**, e.g., "Ask Noa to count the stairs." This targets the co-use benefit without requiring the parent to sit through every session.
- Sibling and social features: a family "garden" where each child's progress contributes, cooperative rather than competitive. An older sibling can "teach" a younger one via a supervised mode.

### Gaps
- No direct evidence was found on metacognitive prompts inside apps for ages 4-6.
- The current EEF parental-engagement months figure was not verified.
- No Israeli-specific parent-involvement data was gathered.

## 7. Concrete product rules and mechanics derived from the research

### Takeaway
Translate the evidence into a small set of explicit, tunable rules. Use a success-rate target band that is age-dependent and higher for younger children. Space reviews across days on a fixed expanding ladder. Scaffold with contingent hints that fade. Mix in review. Give immediate, task-focused feedback. Keep sessions short, then end. Use guided-play framing and parent co-use. Every number below is either a sourced anchor or a clearly labeled design default for A/B tuning.

### Cited Findings
(Each rule lists its evidentiary anchor. The rules themselves are design inferences.)
- Success-rate anchors: 85% (theory, [Wilson et al. 2019](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41467-019-12552-4)); about 80% (guided practice, [Rosenshine 2012](https://www.aft.org/sites/default/files/periodicals/Rosenshine.pdf)); 75% default with 60/75/90% options (Math Garden, [ERIC](https://eric.ed.gov/?id=EJ925823) / [Jansen 2013](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S1041608012001951)); Kumon starts at near-100% (full marks) ([Kumon](https://www.kumongroup.com/eng/about-kumon/method/small-steps/)).
- Spacing anchors: gap of about 10-20% of the desired retention interval ([Cepeda 2008](https://doi.org/10.1111/j.1467-9280.2008.02209.x)); expanding ≈ uniform ([Latimier 2021](http://www.lscp.net/persons/ramus/docs/EPR20.pdf)); spaced exposure works at age 2 ([Childers & Tomasello 2002](https://doi.org/10.1037/0012-1649.38.6.967)).
- Scheduler anchors: SM-2 intervals of 1 and 6 days, then ×EF (2.5) ([SM-2](https://super-memory.com/english/ol/sm2.htm)); FSRS target R=0.9 ([FSRS wiki](https://github.com/open-spaced-repetition/awesome-fsrs/wiki/The-Algorithm)); HLR p=2^(−Δ/h) ([Settles & Meeder](https://research.duolingo.com/papers/settles.acl16.pdf)).
- Mastery anchors: BKT P(L)≥0.95 ([Corbett & Anderson 1995](https://doi.org/10.1007/BF01099821)); Kumon requires all problems correct ([Kumon](https://www.kumon.com/ca-en/about-kumon/kumon-method/small-steps/five-features)).

### Inferences
**A. Difficulty and adaptivity**
1. **Target success band by age** (design defaults): 2.5 yrs about 90-95%, 5.5 yrs about 85-90%, 8.5 yrs about 75-85%. Expose a "challenge dial" (easy / just right / hard, mapped to about 90 / 80 / 70%) for the 8.5-year-old, as Math Garden does. Rationale: younger children need more success for persistence (Jansen 2013, Kumon). The 85% rule is a ceiling on difficulty, not a floor.
2. **Item selection:** maintain Elo θ per child per domain and β per item. Choose items with predicted P(correct) in the band, with occasional (about 10%) "stretch" items 10-15 points of probability harder and "confidence" items that are easier. Use K = K0/(1+0.05·n) with uncertainty decaying by attempts (Pelánek form). Never show the same item twice in a row.
3. **Warm-up and cool-down:** the first 1-2 items of each session are review items at about 95% predicted success. The last item is chosen to be likely correct (end on success).
4. **Frustration guard:** after 2 consecutive errors, drop difficulty a step and offer a hint. After 3 errors on a skill, switch to a worked example or a different skill. Never let a 2.5- or 5.5-year-old fail more than 3 times in a row.
5. **"I don't know" button** (Math Garden's "?"): no penalty. It shows a worked example and then a near-identical item. This reduces guessing and supports learning from errors.
6. **Response time:** ignore RT for scoring under age 6. For age 8+, an optional "speed round" mode uses an HSHS-like score for fluency facts (e.g., ×-tables) only after accuracy mastery. Display time as a personal best, not as a countdown penalty.

**B. Mastery and progression**
7. **Mastery rule** (default): BKT P(L)≥0.95, *or* at least 85% correct over the last 8 attempts spread over **2 or more separate days**, with hint-free answers on at least the last 3. Mastery unlocks the next node in the skill graph.
8. **Tiny steps** (Kumon): each new item type differs from the previous by one feature (bigger numbers *or* new format, not both). New skill introduction follows the sequence: a model, then 2-3 guided items, then independent items.
9. **Concreteness fading** (C→P→A): for each math concept, levels progress from manipulative or drag objects, to pictures and dots, to symbols. A lapse falls back one representation level.

**C. Spacing and review**
10. **Review ladder for mastered skills:** 1 day, 3 days, 7 days, 14 days, 30 days, 60 days. A correct review promotes and a lapse demotes two rungs. Alternatively, use HLR or FSRS-lite targeting recall ≈0.85-0.9 once enough data exists.
11. **Session composition** (5.5- and 8.5-year-olds): about 60% current-edge practice, 30% spaced review of due skills (interleaved), and 10% preview or stretch. For the 2.5-year-old, use about 50% familiar favorites (repetition is enjoyable at this age) and 50% gentle novelty.
12. **Interleave** only after each skill in a mix is individually above about 70%. Interleave similar-looking categories (e.g., ב/כ, ד/ר, +/− word problems), because discrimination is where interleaving pays off (Brunmair & Richter).
13. **Garden mechanic** (Math Garden-inspired): each skill is a plant that visibly grows with practice and droops when a review is due. This creates a non-punitive, visible cue for spaced return. Plants never die.

**D. Feedback**
14. Give feedback **immediately** for all ages. Correct: brief, specific praise ("You counted every one!"). Wrong: show the correct answer or the reasoning (e.g., animate the counting), then present a similar item soon (within 1-3 items).
15. Feedback is task- and process-focused, never person-focused ("You're so smart" is out; "You tried a new way" is in).
16. For age 8+, allow one self-correction attempt before revealing the answer. This is a mild desirable difficulty.

**E. Session, timing, frequency**
17. Session caps: about 5 min (2.5 yrs), about 12 min (5.5 yrs) and about 20 min (8.5 yrs), with a soft prompt to stop and a "come back tomorrow" plant-watering hook. Daily total for the 2.5- and 5.5-year-olds stays well within the AAP 1-hour guidance.
18. Frequency: daily short sessions beat weekend binges. The app nudges at most one session per day and no notifications to the child.
19. Fatigue detection: if accuracy drops below the band for 4 or more items, or RT rises sharply, offer a break and end on an easy win.

**F. Play, layout, multimodality**
20. Guided play framing: a story or quest goal with child choice of which activity or "plant" to do next (agency). The system sets the learning goal, as in Weisberg et al.
21. Clean screens: one concept per screen. Decoration stays outside the task area, and task images are simple and relevant (Fisher 2014; Kaminski & Sloutsky 2013).
22. Dual coding: every instruction is spoken in Hebrew audio plus an icon. This is essential for pre-readers. Words are paired with pictures, without redundant on-screen text for non-readers.
23. Embodied mechanics: linear number tracks with hop and drag, counting with finger taps, letter-tracing with a finger, and movement prompts ("jump 3 times") for the 2.5- and 5.5-year-olds.
24. Four Pillars checklist for every activity: is it minds-on, free of distracting rewards, meaningful (connected to the child's world), and does it have a social hook (parent or sibling prompt)?

**G. Motivation, metacognition, parents**
25. Rewards: stickers and coins mark *effort and progress* (sessions completed, skills grown, comebacks). They should be unpredictable and celebratory rather than a per-answer payment. Avoid leaderboards between siblings of different ages and use a cooperative family garden instead.
26. Self-rating (5.5+): three faces at session end. For age 8+, a "predict your score" prompt compared with the actual score, to build calibration.
27. Parent mode: daily 2-line summary, one offline activity suggestion, and co-play prompts for the 2.5-year-old. Parents can adjust the challenge dial and session caps.
28. Explicit celebration of mistakes as learning ("Mistakes help your brain grow" plus a quick retry), consistent with error-correction evidence (Metcalfe 2017).

### Gaps
- All numeric defaults above (bands, caps, ladder intervals, mastery counts) are syntheses of the anchors, not values validated for ages 2-10. They should be A/B-tuned with logged data, e.g., by tracking persistence (sessions per week) against learning (delayed review accuracy).
- No evidence was found on the optimal interleaving ratio for children under 8.
