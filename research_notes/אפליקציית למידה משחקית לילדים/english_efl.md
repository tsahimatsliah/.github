## 1. Israeli Ministry of Education English curriculum (Foundation level, Pre-A1/A1, grades 1-3, Can-Dos, Band I, literacy)

### Takeaway
English is a compulsory subject in Israel from grade 3 to grade 12. An early-English program, piloted in 2022-23 and then extended down to grade 2 and grade 1 (with "ENJOY ENGLISH" in kindergarten and grade 1), means many children now meet English earlier. The 2018/2020 curriculum maps "Pre-foundation" to CEFR Pre-A1, covering early literacy (letters, upper and lower case) and Pre-Band I vocabulary. "Foundation" maps to A1/Band I, which the 2020 table places at grades 4-6, with at least 1,200 lexical items by the end of Foundation. A grade-3 child is therefore at the Pre-A1 stage: letters, sounds, decoding, and Pre-Band I oral vocabulary.

### Cited Findings
- The 2018 revision has three levels: Foundation, Intermediate and Proficiency. They are cumulative: Foundation lexis is included in Intermediate, and Intermediate lexis in Proficiency. — [Revised English Curriculum Including Band III Lexis (2018)](https://meyda.education.gov.il/files/Mazkirut_Pedagogit/English/Curriculum2018July.pdf)
- The Foundation Level lexis document says learners know "at least 1200 items, including the core items in Band I." — [Foundation Level: Lexis](https://meyda.education.gov.il/files/Mazkirut_Pedagogit/English/band11July18.pdf)

Only its cover was visible in search. — [Knesset RIC](https://fs.knesset.gov.il/globaldocs/MMM/98dbe54f-3fa7-e611-80cb-00155d0a95aa/2_98dbe54f-3fa7-e611-80cb-00155d0a95aa_11_8832.pdf)

### Inferences
- **8.5-year-old (grade 3):** Target the Ministry's Pre-A1 Can-Dos: recognizing and naming upper- and lowercase letters, letter-sound correspondences, decoding simple CVC words, a core set of high-frequency/sight words, Pre-Band I oral vocabulary, and simple "I can" spoken phrases (greetings, introducing self, likes). Grade 3 is the first compulsory year, so school will be covering the same ground. The game should support and reinforce school work, not race ahead of it.
- **5.5-year-old (kindergarten, "gan chova"):** The relevant frame is ENJOY ENGLISH-style oral exposure. That means listening, songs, simple vocabulary and routines, without formal reading. Optional letter-sound play can be added.
- **2.5-year-old:** No curriculum applies. Use exposure only (see section 6).
- Cambridge Pre A1 Starters lists can stand in for the Ministry list while the Ministry PDF is unavailable. Both sit at Pre-A1, so overlap is likely for core topics (colors, numbers, family, animals, body, food, school objects, toys, clothes). The overlap has not been verified.

## 2. Early EFL research: age effects, input quantity, oral-first, songs/chants, TPR, storytelling, CLIL

### Takeaway
In a school foreign-language setting, starting early gives no automatic advantage. Muñoz's Barcelona Age Factor project found that older starters progress faster in the short term, and that the amount and quality of input matter at least as much as starting age. An early start only pays off with substantially more exposure. For young children, multimodal methods work in small studies: songs plus TPR beat either one alone. Live social interaction matters greatly for the youngest learners.

### Cited Findings
- Muñoz argues that "the amount and quality of the input bear a significant influence on the effects that age of initial learning has on second language learning." — [Muñoz, GALA invited paper](https://www.enl.auth.gr/gala/14th/papers/invited%20speakers/Munoz.pdf)
- BAF findings: older learners' faster progress in school is attributed to cognitive development, which lets them benefit more from explicit instruction. Younger learners in school do not get the quantity and quality of exposure typical of natural acquisition. The conclusion is that earlier instruction helps only with more exposure time or other supports. The project ran for 9 years with more than 1,700 learners in Catalan state schools. — [Muñoz, GALA paper](https://www.enl.auth.gr/gala/14th/papers/invited%20speakers/Munoz.pdf); [Age and the Rate of Foreign Language Learning (2006)](https://www.degruyterbrill.com/document/doi/10.21832/9781853598937/html)
- Key follow-up publications: Muñoz (2011, IRAL), "Is input more significant than starting age in foreign language acquisition?"; Muñoz (2014, *Applied Linguistics*) on starting age vs. input effects on oral performance. — [Muñoz GALA paper (references)](https://www.enl.auth.gr/gala/14th/papers/invited%20speakers/Munoz.pdf)
- Barcelona English Language Corpus (BELC): an open corpus of the BAF learners' oral and written English. — [TalkBank BELC](https://slabank.talkbank.org/access/English/BELC.html)
- A study of foreign-language phonology in formal settings examined age effects on pronunciation. Title and existence confirmed; results not retrieved. — [EHU record](https://ekoizpen-zientifikoa.ehu.eus/documentos/63930ba07a05941066fa7a94)
- Songs and TPR: 72 Thai preschoolers aged 4-5 learned 12 target words over 6 weeks. Songs, TPR and the combination all significantly improved vocabulary, and the combination (TPR&S) had the strongest effect. — [Magnussen & Sukying 2021, Thai TESOL Journal](https://so05.tci-thaijo.org/index.php/thaitesoljournal/article/view/252356)
- Personalized TPR outperformed traditional TPR for recalling English word meanings among higher-level Taiwanese EFL students. — [SAGE Open 2024 (DOAJ)](https://doaj.org/article/aed415c0121141689a3ddb6f0506f686)
- Other TPR studies (Indonesia, Vietnam, Mexico) are small, often one-group pre/post designs. The Vietnamese study notes that few studies exist on TPR's effect on young learners' vocabulary. — [VNU study](https://js.vnu.edu.vn/ER/article/view/4573/3981); [BUAP thesis](https://repositorioinstitucional.buap.mx/items/ba16f4ec-d12d-4a75-a438-256d93d6eb38/full)
- Songs help young learners retain vocabulary and let them repeat and memorize chunks of language. — [IDEAS journal](https://ejournal.iainpalopo.ac.id/index.php/ideas/article/download/10099/7749/53931)

### Inferences
- For a home game, the Muñoz finding favors high-frequency, short, daily exposure (minutes per day, every day) over occasional long sessions. The total amount of meaningful input matters most.
- An oral-first sequence (listen, understand, say, then read) suits the 2.5- and 5.5-year-olds. The 8.5-year-old can benefit from explicit instruction, such as phonics rules and pattern explanations in Hebrew, because BAF links older learners' advantage to explicit learning.
- Songs combined with actions (TPR) is the method with the best small-study support for 4-5-year-olds, which is the 5.5-year-old's band.
- Storytelling and CLIL: no specific studies were retrieved this session (see Gaps). Simple "content" themes (animals, colors in art, counting) fit the CLIL idea naturally.

### Gaps
- No primary sources were retrieved this session for Cameron (*Teaching Languages to Young Learners*, 2001), Pinter (*Teaching Young Language Learners*), storybook-reading vocabulary studies (repeated reading, number of exposures), or CLIL with young learners. The second half of the search query (storybook reading) returned nothing usable.
- No effect sizes from the BAF studies were obtained, only qualitative conclusions.

## 3. Teaching English reading to Hebrew-speaking kids: cross-linguistic issues, phonics programs, sight words, decodables

### Takeaway
Hebrew readers come from a consonant-based, right-to-left script. Children's Hebrew is pointed (nikud), which is a fairly shallow orthography; adult Hebrew is unpointed and deep. English adds left-to-right reading, vowels written as ordinary letters, and an inconsistent deep orthography. Israeli research shows that English letter knowledge at the start of the first EFL year is the strongest predictor of later EFL reading and spelling, more than Hebrew L1 skills. Pronunciation pressure points are /θ ð/ (th), /w/ (said as /v/), tense/lax vowel pairs (ship/sheep), and vowels generally. Systematic phonics has strong general evidence, roughly +5 months' progress, with larger effects for younger children.

### Cited Findings
- Kahn-Horwitz, Shimron & Sparks (2006), 145 Israeli 4th graders: knowledge of English letters played a more prominent role than Hebrew L1 components in separating strong from weak EFL readers. SES separated groups for word recognition but not for comprehension. — [Annals of Dyslexia 2006](https://springerlink.bibliotecabuap.elogim.com/doi/10.1007/s11881-006-0007-1)
- Kahn-Horwitz, Sparks & Goldstein (2012), longitudinal (grades 4, 9, 12): EFL letter knowledge at the start of the first EFL year (grade 4 at that time) predicted end-of-first-year EFL spelling. — [Applied Psycholinguistics](https://resolve.cambridge.org/core/journals/applied-psycholinguistics/article/english-as-a-foreign-language-spelling-development-a-longitudinal-study/15A199C411FBADF17878711DA6E1C9CF)
- Kahn-Horwitz (2016): many Israeli EFL teachers, mostly non-native speakers, lack orthographic content knowledge of English. A course improved it. — [Annals of Dyslexia 2016](https://springerlink.bibliotecabuap.elogim.com/10.1007/s11881-015-0120-0)
- Unpointed Hebrew is a deep orthography, since readers must retrieve vowels from memory. In English, vowels are letters visually equal to consonants. Hebrew-speaking students may need an "approach shift" in word recognition for English, and this ability may predict difficulties. — [Kahn-Horwitz, Shimron & Sparks (Springer)](https://link.springer.com/doi/10.1007/s11145-010-9235-1); [Reading & Writing 2005](https://springerlink.bibliotecabuap.elogim.com/content/pdf/10.1007/s11145-005-3179-x.pdf)
- Saiegh-Haddad: Hebrew speakers' phonological awareness rests on a language-specific "body-coda" syllable representation, which may affect how they segment English syllables and onsets/rimes. — [Frontiers in Psychology 2019](https://www.frontiersin.org/articles/10.3389/fpsyg.2019.01809/pdf)
- In deep orthographies like English, phonological awareness predicts reading longer than in shallow orthographies. — [Frontiers 2019](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/psychology/articles/10.3389/fpsyg.2019.01809/pdf)
- Pronunciation pressure points:
  - Hebrew does not distinguish tense from lax vowels, so pairs like hit/heat and cook/kook are hard. — [Non-native pronunciations of English (Wikipedia mirror)](https://akarinohon.com/text/taketori.cgi/en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Non-native_pronunciations_of_English)
  - Basson's CUNY dissertation (1980s) found that Hebrew speakers did poorly across all English vowels and centralized the back vowels. — [same](https://akarinohon.com/text/taketori.cgi/en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Non-native_pronunciations_of_English)
  - /θ/ and /ð/ do not exist in Hebrew, and t/th confusion is common. /w/ is often said as /v/ ("white" as "vite"). — [BoldVoice blog](https://boldvoice.com/blog/english-pronunciation-hebrew-speakers); [IBTimes](https://www.ibtimes.co.uk/10-mistakes-israelis-make-when-speaking-english-1743958) (both popular/anecdotal sources)
  - Hebrew stress usually falls on the final or penultimate syllable and can carry over into English. — [PronunciationPro Hebrew guide](https://cdn.pronunciationpro.com/wp-content/uploads/2020/08/Hebrew-NEW.pdf)
- EEF Teaching and Learning Toolkit: phonics adds about +5 months' progress on average, has a moderate impact, very low cost, and very extensive evidence. Older toolkit versions (2013-14) said +4 months. Most studies involve intensive small-group or one-to-one support. — [EEF Phonics](https://educationendowmentfoundation.org.uk/education-evidence/teaching-learning-toolkit/phonics)
- Program-specific results vary. An EEF trial of Flexible Phonics found 1 month *less* progress in word recognition than the comparison group. — [EEF Flexible Phonics](https://educationendowmentfoundation.org.uk/projects-and-evaluation/projects/flexible-phonics)
- Ehri (2003) meta-analysis, cited in an RCT: the phonics effect is larger for younger children (K-grade 1, d = 0.55) than older ones (grades 2-6, d = 0.27). — [Patel et al. 2022, JCAL](https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/10.1111/jcal.12592)
- EEF early-years literacy guidance stresses "more than just phonics": oral language, vocabulary and shared reading alongside phonics. — [EEF Literacy in the early years](https://educationendowmentfoundation.org.uk/early-years/more-than-just-phonics)

### Inferences
- **Grade 3 (8.5):** Letter-sound knowledge is the key lever, per the Israeli evidence. Teach a synthetic-phonics sequence explicitly: single letter sounds, then CVC blending (s-a-t), digraphs (sh, ch, th, ck), then long vowels and magic e. Deliberately contrast vowel pairs (short a/e/i/o/u, ship/sheep). Add a small, cumulative set of high-frequency irregular "tricky words" (the, I, you, is, are, my, said...). Use decodable texts that contain only taught patterns.
- Program choice is less important than following a systematic sequence. Jolly Phonics, Letterland, Read Write Inc. and UFLI all follow systematic synthetic phonics with similar letter-sound orders. Note that this summary of the programs comes from background knowledge and was not verified this session.
- Build left-to-right tracking into the interface: a highlight or finger moving left to right during blending. Explicitly show b/d/p/q differences, which are common confusions for new readers of Latin script.
- Phoneme work for a Hebrew-speaking child should include: /w/ vs /v/ (wet/vet), /θ/ vs /t/ and /s/ (three/tree), /ð/ vs /d/ (this/dis), short vs long vowels, and final-syllable stress habits.
- **5.5-year-old:** Oral phonological awareness in English (rhyme, first sound, "I spy") and letter-sound play are enough. Formal decoding is probably premature while Hebrew reading is also just starting.

### Gaps
- No primary source was retrieved this session on the specific content or sequencing of Jolly Phonics (42 sounds), Letterland, UFLI or Read Write Inc. Program details above are background knowledge only.
- The Dolch (220 service words plus 95 nouns) and Fry (1,000) list facts were not verified this session.
- No direct study was found on English vowel awareness in Hebrew-speaking children.
- No study was found on teaching English reading to Hebrew speakers in grades 1-3 under the new earlier start. The Israeli studies cover grade 4 as the starting point.
- No research was found on interference between learning to read Hebrew (pointed) and English in the same year.

## 4. Evidence on digital English learning apps for kids

### Takeaway
Rigorous evidence is thin. Khan Academy Kids has the only small RCT among the major apps (preschoolers moved from the 34th to 47th percentile after 10 weeks). Lingokids and Duolingo ABC rely on company-funded pre/post or observational studies. Teach Your Monster to Read has no independent outcome evaluation that could be found; the evidence is testimonial. Meta-analyses put computer-supported early literacy effects at about d = 0.29 and touchscreen learning at about d = 0.46 for ages 0-5. Digital is a useful supplement, but expect modest effects. A game-based phonics RCT with ELL first and second graders in India is directly relevant to EFL.

### Cited Findings
- **Khan Academy Kids:** an RCT with 50 preschoolers from high-poverty families, compared against art or music apps. With at least 1 hour per week over 10 weeks, children moved from the 34th to the 47th national percentile on the Test of Preschool Early Literacy. The control group made no significant gain in phonological awareness. Published in the *Journal of Children and Media*. It is small and does not identify which features worked. — [Khan Academy blog](https://blog.khanacademy.org/khan-academy-kids-improves-pre-literacy-skills-in-preschoolers-research-confirms/); [EdWeek](https://www.edweek.org/leadership/khan-app-boosted-early-literacy-parent-teaching-in-small-trial/2020/05); [ResearchGate RCT](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/348435383_A_randomized_controlled_trial_of_an_educational_app_to_improve_preschoolers'_emergent_literacy_skills)
- **Lingokids:** a UC Davis study with 35 children aged 3-5 over 8 weeks. Early literacy improved 7.6% for Plus users vs. 2.5% for Basic users. This compares paid and free tiers, not app vs. no app, and is company-promoted. — [Lingokids blog](https://lingokids.com/blog/posts/proven-results-kids-learn-faster-with-lingokids-plus); [GlobeNewswire](https://www.globenewswire.com/news-release/2024/11/21/2984859/0/en/lingokids-demonstrates-effective-child-learning-in-just-8-weeks-results-from-an-efficacy-study.html); [Lingokids Research](https://lingokids.com/research)
- **Duolingo ABC:** an EDC formative study (January 2022). The report says the app "holds promise." The main analysis was whether hours of use predicted post-test scores, not a randomized comparison. The marketing claim of a "28% literacy gain" in 9 weeks is not clearly backed by a control group. Note that Duolingo ABC is designed for native English-speaking children learning to read, not for EFL. — [EDC report](https://duolingo-papers.s3.amazonaws.com/reports/edc-report-on-duolingo-abc.pdf); [Duolingo efficacy studies](https://www.duolingo.com/efficacy/studies); [Duolingo ABC](https://abc.duolingo.com/)
- **Teach Your Monster to Read:** developed with academics at the University of Roehampton and funded by the Usborne Foundation. Only teacher and parent testimonials were found, such as a Year 3 pupil's "dramatic improvement" in phonics engagement. No controlled outcome data was found. An independent literacy review calls it one of the few programs closely aligned with the Science of Reading. Several "study finds" pages in search results are spam with no identifiable study. — [teachyourmonster.org](https://www.teachyourmonster.org/monster-news/using-the-game-in-school-and-at-home/); [Modulo review](https://www.modulo.app/all-resources/teachyourmonstertoread)
- **Game-based phonics RCT with ELL children:** first and second graders learning English in India. — [Patel et al. 2022, Journal of Computer Assisted Learning](https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/10.1111/jcal.12592) (abstract-level only; effect sizes not retrieved)
- **Meta-analyses:**
  - Computer-supported early literacy interventions in preschool and kindergarten: average effect size 0.29, small compared with non-digital phonological awareness interventions. — [ScienceDirect meta-analysis](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S1747938X1930394X)
  - Touchscreen learning, ages 0-5: d = 0.46. — [PMC6305619](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC6305619/)
  - ICT in early childhood and primary education: positive overall, especially for language skills (effect size 0.24). — [PMC12213516](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC12213516/)

### Inferences
- Common design features of the better-supported apps: a systematic phonics sequence (Teach Your Monster to Read, Khan Kids), short sessions, a character or narrative wrapper, and immediate audio feedback. A custom game should copy these structural features rather than rely on any single brand.
- Most of these apps assume English-speaking or ELL children immersed in English. A Hebrew-speaking EFL child lacks the oral vocabulary those apps take for granted. The game should teach word meaning (picture plus audio, Hebrew support when needed) before or alongside decoding.
- An effect of about d = 0.3 is meaningful but modest. Combining the app with parent co-play (singing the songs together, reading the story together) is likely to matter, as the Kuhl findings in section 6 suggest.

### Gaps
- No evidence was retrieved this session on Novakid (live tutor platform), Reading Eggs, Starfall, Endless Alphabet/Endless Reader, Homer, Epic, or Fish School.
- Effect sizes for the Patel 2022 RCT were not retrieved.
- No study was found of any of these apps with Hebrew-speaking or Israeli children.

## 5. Speech recognition and pronunciation feedback for kids

### Takeaway
Automatic speech recognition (ASR) is much less accurate on children: roughly 15-21% word error rate (WER) at ages 6-10 and up to 25-35% for kindergartners, vs. about 5% for adults. Non-native (Hebrew-accented) child speech is likely to be harder still. Scoring pronunciation in a way that punishes the child is risky. Use speech input generously (accept approximate attempts) or as record-and-compare, not strict scoring.

### Cited Findings
- Commercial-style speech-to-text reaches about 5% WER on adults but about 11-18% on children in general, about 15-21% at ages 6-10, and up to 35% for kindergartners. Fine-tuning on child data gives 12-30% relative WER reductions. — [The Learning Agency, Closing the Child Speech Recognition Gap](https://the-learning-agency.com/guides-resources/closing-the-child-speech-recognition-gap-evidence-limitations-and-paths-forward/)
- An LREC 2020 child-adapted model reached 29% WER, down from 62% for an adult-trained baseline. Child speech varies more within and across speakers, and age-typical mispronunciations get confused with recognizer errors. — [LREC 2020](https://aclanthology.org/2020.lrec-1.778.pdf)
- In a kindergarten reading context, WER reached 25% even for isolated single words. — [On the Difficulties of ASR for Kindergarten-Aged Children](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/327389064_On_the_Difficulties_of_Automatic_Speech_Recognition_for_Kindergarten-Aged_Children)
- Research pronunciation-feedback systems model child-specific phoneme errors. K-Function (2025 preprint) reports a 1.39% phoneme error rate on the MyST corpus, but MyST is native-English children aged about 8-10, a narrower case. — [K-Function arXiv 2507.03043](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2507.03043)
- Proposals to report phoneme error rate alongside WER for child fluency assessment. — [arXiv 2505.19671](https://arxiv.org/html/2505.19671v1)
- Toddler pronunciation evaluation (Korean, 2026 preprint) uses diarization plus self-supervised learning. — [arXiv 2606.10213](https://arxiv.org/html/2606.10213v1)

### Inferences
- **2.5 and 5.5-year-olds:** Avoid ASR-graded tasks. Use "say it" prompts where any vocalization earns praise and the model sound plays again (echo), or have a parent confirm.
- **8.5-year-old:** Limited ASR is possible for single target words with a small constrained vocabulary (for example, picking among 3 expected words), not open-ended transcription. Accept near matches. Never show "wrong" for pronunciation. Target sounds like /w/ and /θ/ are better handled through listening-discrimination games (minimal pairs: tap "wet" or "vet") than through production scoring.
- Record-and-playback ("hear yourself next to the character") gives useful self-monitoring without any ASR accuracy risk.

### Gaps
- No ASR accuracy data was found for non-native (Hebrew-L1) child speakers of English.
- No evaluation was found of current 2026 commercial APIs (for example, Whisper variants or Azure pronunciation assessment) on young children. Figures vary widely by dataset and noise.

## 6. Toddler bilingual exposure at 2.5: what is realistic and beneficial

### Takeaway
Infants and toddlers learn foreign-language sounds from live, socially interactive exposure, not from passive video or audio. Kuhl's 2003 study found 9-10-month-olds learned Mandarin phonetic contrasts from about 5 hours of live play, but not from the same material on DVD or audio. For a 2.5-year-old, the realistic goal is enjoyable familiarity: songs, a few dozen picture words, and routines, ideally with a parent co-playing. Socially contingent formats, such as video chat-like responsiveness, are the open question for screens.

### Cited Findings
- Kuhl, Tsao & Liu (2003, PNAS): 9-10-month-olds showed phonetic learning from live, but not prerecorded, foreign-language exposure. Learning was enhanced by social interaction and did not require long-term listening. — [PNAS record](https://bvbvf1.bib-bvb.de/vufind/PrimoRecord/cdi_pnas_primary_100_15_9096); [UW News 2003](https://www.washington.edu/news/2003/07/14/social-interaction-plays-key-role-in-how-infants-learn-language-studies-show/)
- The DVD/audio group showed no learning of the Mandarin contrasts and paid significantly less attention. Kuhl: "Video plus audio or audio-only presentation did not work for infants 9 and 10 months of age." The study used about 5 hours of exposure in a lab setting. — [UW News](https://www.washington.edu/news/2003/07/14/social-interaction-plays-key-role-in-how-infants-learn-language-studies-show/); [VOA Learning English](https://learningenglish.voanews.com/a/language-study-babies-learn-better-with-others-/4624735.html)
- A 2014 study of 24-30-month-olds learning novel verbs compared live interaction, socially contingent video chat, and non-contingent (yoked) video, testing whether social contingency is the key ingredient. Results were not retrieved in this session. — [PMC3962808](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3962808)
- Touchscreen learning for ages 0-5 shows a positive effect (d = 0.46), but this covers learning in general, not foreign language. — [PMC6305619](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC6305619/)

### Inferences
- **For the 2.5-year-old:** Design for co-play, with the parent and the child together. Use very short sessions (a few minutes), large tap targets, and no failure states. Content should be action songs (head-shoulders style), routines (hello/bye, bath, food, "more"), and about 20-50 picture nouns from the child's world (family members, pets, toys, foods). Family-photo flashcards ("Where's Daddy? Daddy!") add social relevance and contingency.
- Realistic outcomes: understanding words and songs and joining in with actions. Spontaneous English production is a bonus, not a goal.
- Contingency in the app (the character reacts immediately to the child's tap or voice, then waits) may partly approximate social interaction. This is an assumption, since the 2014 results were not retrieved.

### Gaps
- No research was retrieved this session on screen-time guidance (for example, AAP/WHO limits for under-3s or 2-5-year-olds) or on Hebrew-English bilingual toddlers specifically.
- No evidence was found on how many English exposure hours per week produce measurable gains at 2-3 years in a foreign-language (non-immersion) setting.

## 7. Concrete game-mechanic ideas mapped to evidence and age

### Takeaway
Map each age to a different core loop. **2.5:** co-play songs, routines and family-photo vocabulary with no failure states. **5.5:** songs plus TPR actions, listen-and-tap vocabulary, rhyme and first-sound games, mini-dialogues with a character. **8.5:** a systematic phonics path (letter-sound, blending machine, decodable stories), minimal-pair listening for Hebrew-specific sounds, sight-word review, and Pre-A1 Can-Do dialogues. All three use spaced review and daily short sessions, because input quantity is what drives results.

### Cited Findings
- Songs combined with TPR is better than either alone for 4-5-year-olds. — [Magnussen & Sukying 2021](https://so05.tci-thaijo.org/index.php/thaitesoljournal/article/view/252356)
- Personalization improved TPR vocabulary recall. — [SAGE Open 2024](https://doaj.org/article/aed415c0121141689a3ddb6f0506f686)
- English letter knowledge is the top predictor of Israeli EFL reading and spelling success. — [Kahn-Horwitz et al. 2006](https://springerlink.bibliotecabuap.elogim.com/doi/10.1007/s11881-006-0007-1); [Kahn-Horwitz et al. 2012](https://resolve.cambridge.org/core/journals/applied-psycholinguistics/article/english-as-a-foreign-language-spelling-development-a-longitudinal-study/15A199C411FBADF17878711DA6E1C9CF)
- Phonics has larger effects for younger children (d = 0.55, K-1). — [Patel et al. 2022 citing Ehri 2003](https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/10.1111/jcal.12592)
- Live, social interaction matters for very young learners. — [UW News on Kuhl 2003](https://www.washington.edu/news/2003/07/14/social-interaction-plays-key-role-in-how-infants-learn-language-studies-show/)
- ASR is unreliable for young children's speech. — [The Learning Agency](https://the-learning-agency.com/guides-resources/closing-the-child-speech-recognition-gap-evidence-limitations-and-paths-forward/)
- Input quantity determines whether an early start pays off. — [Muñoz](https://www.enl.auth.gr/gala/14th/papers/invited%20speakers/Munoz.pdf)
- Pre-A1 Can-Do: recognizing letters in upper and lower case. — [Ministry Pre-A1 Can-Dos](https://meyda.education.gov.il/files/Mazkirut_Pedagogit/English/CurriculumFilesAugust21/candospreA1.pdf)

### Inferences
These are design proposals derived from the findings above, not tested mechanics.

**Age 2.5 (exposure, co-play)**
- *Sing-and-do:* An animated character sings a short action song, and the child copies the action. The parent mode has a "do it together" prompt. (Based on songs + TPR and the need for social interaction.)
- *Family photo peekaboo:* Family photos hide behind doors; tap one and the app says "Mommy!", "Grandpa!", "Doggy!" with Hebrew echo optional. (Based on personalization and social relevance.)
- *Routine scenes:* bath, food, bedtime. Tap objects to hear "cup", "ball", "more", "all done". No wrong answers.

**Age 5.5 (oral vocabulary and phonological awareness)**
- *Listen-and-tap:* Hear "Find the red apple" and tap among 2-4 pictures. Distractors increase as accuracy rises. (Builds receptive vocabulary first, following oral-first.)
- *Simon Says / TPR:* The character gives commands ("jump", "touch your nose") and the parent confirms. Optionally, the device camera or accelerometer can detect motion.
- *Rhyme train and first-sound fishing:* "Which one starts with /b/?" Catch the fish with a ball picture.
- *Mini-dialogues with a mascot:* scripted exchanges ("Hello! What's your name?" "I'm Noa"). The child taps a speech bubble or speaks; any attempt is accepted.
- *Song karaoke:* lyrics with pictures, and the child fills in the missing word.

**Age 8.5 / grade 3 (Pre-A1 literacy, aligned with school)**
- *Letter-sound path:* one new sound per session in a systematic order. Show upper and lower case together, with a Hebrew-letter analogy where helpful (for example, m is like מ).
- *Blending machine:* Drag letter tiles into a machine; it says each sound left to right and then the blended word (s-a-t → sat), and a picture appears if the word is real. Add a "real or silly word" sorter.
- *Minimal-pair ears:* Hear "wet" and tap the wet picture or the vet picture. Contrasts include w/v, th/t (three/tree), th/d, ship/sheep, cap/cup. Listening first, because ASR is unreliable.
- *Tricky-word catcher:* high-frequency irregular words on falling bubbles, with spaced repetition.
- *Decodable comic stories:* 3-6 panels using only taught patterns, with a click-to-hear word fallback.
- *Can-Do quests:* "Introduce yourself to the robot" (name, age, likes), mapped to Pre-A1/A1 Can-Do statements.
- *Record and compare:* The child records a word and plays it next to the model, with no automatic score.

**Cross-cutting**
- A spaced-review scheduler (Leitner-style) for all vocabulary and letter sounds.
- Short daily sessions with a streak, because input quantity is what drives results.
- Hebrew UI and instructions with English content. Hebrew scaffolding should fade over time.
- A shared family mode where the older sibling "teaches" the younger one, such as reading a word to them.

### Gaps
- No studies were retrieved on spaced repetition for young children's L2 vocabulary specifically, or on the optimal number of exposures per new word.
- No evidence was found on Hebrew-language scaffolding vs. English-only instructions in EFL apps for young children.
- No evidence was found on sibling or peer co-play effects in language apps.
