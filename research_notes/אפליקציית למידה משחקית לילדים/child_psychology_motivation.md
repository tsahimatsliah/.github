## 1. Self-Determination Theory, intrinsic vs extrinsic motivation, overjustification, and when rewards help vs hurt

### Takeaway
Expected, tangible, task-contingent rewards ("do X and you'll get a prize") reliably undermine young children's later free-choice interest in an activity they already liked; unexpected rewards and informational (competence-signalling) feedback do not. Design the game so the activity itself is the reward, and use rewards as surprise, informational acknowledgements rather than as contracts — while noting the Cameron & Pierce camp argues the undermining effect is narrower than SDT proponents claim.

### Cited Findings
- **Lepper, Greene & Nisbett (1973)**, J. Personality & Social Psychology 28:129-137: 51 preschoolers (ages ~3-4) already interested in drawing with markers were split into expected-reward (told in advance they'd get a "Good Player"-type certificate), unexpected-reward, and no-reward groups. In a later free-play session, the **expected-reward group drew less** than before; the unexpected-reward and no-reward groups did not differ significantly. — [Psychology Notes HQ summary](https://www.psychologynoteshq.com/overjustification-effect/); [typeset.io copy of original paper](https://typeset.io/pdf/undermining-children-s-intrinsic-interest-with-extrinsic-3qn44yqiou.pdf)
- Caveats on Lepper 1973: single study, preschoolers, symbolic reward, an already-loved activity; effect depends on reward type, expectancy, contingency and initial interest. — [atticusli.com replication-crisis note](https://atticusli.com/replication-crisis/overjustification-effect/)
- **Deci, Koestner & Ryan (1999)**, Psychological Bulletin 125(6):627-668: meta-analysis of **128 experiments**; authors conclude results strongly support cognitive evaluation theory and that **tangible rewards have a substantial undermining effect** on intrinsic motivation. — [Paper PDF (ubalt.edu)](https://home.ubalt.edu/ntygmitc/642/Articles%20syllabus/Deci%20Koestner%20Ryan%20meta%20IM%20psy%20bull%2099.pdf); [Deci, Koestner & Ryan 2001 education follow-up (Ewha record)](https://pure.ewha.ac.kr/en/publications/extrinsic-rewards-and-intrinsic-motivation-in-education-reconside/)
- Deci et al. 1999 moderator results **[unverified this session — check the paper's tables]**: tangible rewards overall d ≈ -0.34; expected tangible d ≈ -0.36; unexpected tangible ≈ 0 (n.s.); task-noncontingent n.s.; engagement-contingent d ≈ -0.40; completion-contingent d ≈ -0.44; performance-contingent d ≈ -0.28; verbal rewards (positive feedback) *enhanced* free-choice motivation overall (d ≈ +0.33) but the enhancement was significant for college students and **not** for children; tangible-reward undermining was **stronger for children than for college students**. — [Paper PDF](https://home.ubalt.edu/ntygmitc/642/Articles%20syllabus/Deci%20Koestner%20Ryan%20meta%20IM%20psy%20bull%2099.pdf)
- Verbal rewards can themselves become controlling when people act in order to earn praise (controlling vs informational aspect of feedback — the core of cognitive evaluation theory). — [Deci, Koestner & Ryan 2001 (Scribd copy)](https://www.scribd.com/document/335703051/2001-DeciKoestnerRyan-pdf)
- **Debate:** Cameron & Pierce (1994) meta-analysis concluded the undermining effect was minimal and largely inconsequential for educational policy; Deci et al. (1999) argued that meta-analysis was seriously flawed; Cameron & Pierce replied ("Effects of reward on intrinsic motivation — negative, neutral and positive," Psych Bull 1999). Later Cameron-camp work ("Pervasive negative effects of rewards on intrinsic motivation: The myth continues") maintains the effects are conditional, not pervasive. — [PMC review citing the debate](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC2731358/); [ScholarHub record of "The myth continues"](https://scholarhub.vn/publication/:slug/9460423c-1ed9-4760-995d-5dd57e6628aa); [Deci et al. 1999 PDF](https://home.ubalt.edu/ntygmitc/642/Articles%20syllabus/Deci%20Koestner%20Ryan%20meta%20IM%20psy%20bull%2099.pdf)

### Inferences
- **Where both camps agree** (and therefore safe design ground): rewards that are (a) unexpected, (b) not contingent on merely doing the task, and (c) carry competence information are least risky; expected tangible rewards for an activity the child already enjoys are the riskiest. For a learning game the "activity already enjoyed" condition is exactly what we hope to create, so the undermining risk is real.
- **SDT mapping to product** (autonomy / competence / relatedness):
  - Autonomy: offer 2-3 meaningful choices (which world, which character, which activity next), never forced linear sequences; avoid "you must" language; allow skipping.
  - Competence: adaptive difficulty that keeps success high but not trivial; feedback that tells the child what they got better at ("you found 3 rhyming words faster than last time").
  - Relatedness: a warm, consistent companion character; parent and sibling co-play modes; sharing creations with family (not public).
- Informational vs controlling phrasing: "You figured out the pattern!" (informational) vs "Good, you did what I told you — now do 5 more to get your star" (controlling).
- Tangible-like in-app rewards (coins, stickers, trophies) are functionally "tangible" for a 5-year-old; treat them as such.

### Gaps
- Exact Deci et al. 1999 moderator d-values could not be re-confirmed from the full text in this session (fetch blocked).
- No 2020s meta-analysis specifically on digital badges/points for children under 10 was found in this session; the adult/ed-tech gamification literature is mixed.

## 2. Praise research: process vs person praise, growth-mindset interventions and replication debate, phrasing by age

### Takeaway
Praise that labels the child ("you're so smart," "you're a good drawer") makes young children more helpless after setbacks than praise of process/behavior; inflated praise ("incredibly beautiful!") pushes low-self-esteem kids to avoid challenge. Growth-mindset *interventions* show only small average effects (Sisk 2018) and a high-profile replication of Mueller & Dweck failed (Li & Bates 2019) — so use process praise because it is low-cost and sensible, not because it is a proven achievement booster.

### Cited Findings
- **Mueller & Dweck (1998)**, JPSP 75(1):33-52, six studies with 5th graders: intelligence praise led, after failure, to less persistence, less enjoyment, more low-performance goal choices, and attributing failure to low ability; effort-praised children attributed failure to low effort. Nearly 40% (Dweck elsewhere says "more than 40%") of intelligence-praised children **misreported (inflated) their scores** to peers, vs almost none in other groups. — [Mueller & Dweck PDF (Columbia)](https://www.columbia.edu/cu/psychology/courses/3615/Readings/Mueller_Dweck.pdf); [Dweck "The Perils and Promises of Praise" (2007)](https://www.dentonisd.org/cms/lib/TX21000245/Centricity/Domain/6428/The%20Perils%20of%20Promise%20and%20Praise%20by%20Carol%20Dweck.pdf); [progressfocused summary](https://www.progressfocused.com/2020/09/mueller-dweck-1998-classic-study.html)
- **Li & Bates (2019)** — three studies, 624 Chinese children aged 10-13, including a close replication of Mueller & Dweck: praise/mindset manipulation was associated with performance on a moderately difficult post-failure test only marginally (p = .049) and **not with any of the 8 motivation/attribution measures**. Dweck & Yeager responded that it did not meet replication standards; critics note Li & Bates equated effort praise with mindset. — [Li & Bates replication PDF (mrbartonmaths)](https://mrbartonmaths.com/resourcesnew/8.%20Research/Mindset/Mindset%20replication.pdf); [progressfocused "Growth mindset debunked? Not so much"](https://www.progressfocused.com/2020/09/growth-mindset-debunked-not-so-much.html); [Carol Dweck – Wikipedia (debate summary)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Carol_Dweck)
- **Gunderson et al. (2013)**, Child Development: 53 families videotaped at home when children were 1-3; **parents' process praise (not person praise or total praise) predicted children's incremental mindset and challenge-seeking at ages 7-8**. Boys received more process praise than girls (≈24% vs ≈10% of praise). Observational, small sample, questionnaire outcomes. — [PMC full text](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3655123); [UChicago news](https://news.uchicago.edu/article/2013/02/12/parents-who-praise-effort-can-bolster-children-s-persistence-self-belief)
- **Cimpian et al. (2007)**, Psychological Science 18:314-316: 4-year-olds told "**you are a good drawer**" (generic/person label) responded worse after a setback than those told "**you did a good job drawing**" (behavior). — [BPS Research Digest](https://bps.org.uk/research-digest/how-praise-children); [PMC follow-up literature](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6424657)
- Inconsistent praise also matters: "Good job, you're so smart" — mixing praise types affects young children's motivation (Zentall & Morris 2010). — [PubMed 20570281](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/20570281/)
- **Brummelman et al. (2014)**, Psychological Science: adults gave inflated praise to low-self-esteem children about twice as often (33% vs 18%); in an experiment with 240 children aged 8-12, low-self-esteem children given inflated praise ("incredibly beautiful drawing") **chose easier tasks**; high-self-esteem children chose harder ones. Mechanism not directly measured. — [Ohio State news](https://artsandsciences.osu.edu/news/high-praise-could-backfire-kids-low-self-esteem); [BPS Research Digest](https://www.bps.org.uk/research-digest/inflated-praise-your-children-incredibly-bad-idea)
- **Sisk et al. (2018)**, Psychological Science 29(4):549-571: Meta-analysis 1 (k = 273, N = 365,915) — weak mindset–achievement correlation, somewhat stronger in children/adolescents; Meta-analysis 2 (k = 43, N = 57,155) — intervention effects small overall (commonly reported d ≈ 0.08), with somewhat larger effects for academically high-risk and low-SES students; high heterogeneity. — [Sisk et al. PDF (Georgia Tech)](https://englelab.gatech.edu/articles/2018/Sisk,%20Burgoyne%20et%20al.%20(2018)%20-%20Mindset%20and%20Academic%20Achievement.pdf); [ScienceDaily press release](https://sciencedaily.com/releases/2018/05/180522114523.htm); [atticusli summary of d = 0.08](https://atticusli.com/replication-crisis/growth-mindset/)
- Practical guidance: effective verbal praise is specific and behavior-focused. — [Penn State THRIVE](https://thrive.psu.edu/resources/giving-effective-verbal-praise)

### Inferences
- Product position: use **process/behavior praise** (effort, strategy, persistence, improvement) as the default voice of the game; never person/trait labels; never superlatives ("incredible," "genius," "best ever"). Do not market the app as a "growth mindset" intervention — evidence of causal achievement effects in young children is weak.
- **Praise scripts by age (Hebrew-first, audio-delivered for under-7s):**
  - **2.5yo (toddler):** short, warm, descriptive, + sensory celebration. "You found the red ball!" / "!מצאת את הכדור האדום" ; "You tapped the dog — woof!" No evaluation words needed; describe the action. Errors: no "wrong" sound; gently re-model ("Here's the red one — let's tap it together").
  - **5.5yo (boy):** name the strategy/effort. "You kept trying and you got it!" ; "You listened to the first sound — ב like בננה. Smart move" (praise the move, not the kid). After errors: "Tricky one! Let's try a different way." Normalise mistakes: "Mistakes help our brains grow" is fine but keep it light — evidence is modest.
  - **8.5yo (girl):** specific, informational, improvement-referenced, slightly understated (8-10yos detect over-praise). "You solved that in fewer tries than yesterday." ; "That was a hard one and you stuck with it." Offer choice of harder challenge: "Want a tougher version?" Given Gunderson's finding that girls receive less process praise, deliberately ensure parity of process praise across children.
- Avoid praising everything: praise that is constant becomes noise and can be controlling; vary between praise, plain informational feedback ("3 of 4 correct"), and silent success (the satisfying outcome itself).

### Gaps
- No direct studies found on praise delivered by an app/character (vs parent/experimenter) for under-6s; generalisation from adult-delivered praise is an assumption.
- Hebrew-language praise research specific to Israeli families not found.

## 3. Engagement levers: flow, curiosity/information gap, novelty, surprise, variable rewards, collection, narrative, beloved characters, personalization

### Takeaway
The healthiest strong levers are curiosity (moderate, closeable information gaps), a socially contingent beloved character, and light personalization; variable-ratio reward schedules and loot-box-like mechanics are the levers most associated with harm and should be excluded. Character-based learning works better when the child has a parasocial bond and the character responds contingently.

### Cited Findings
- **Loewenstein (1994)**, "The Psychology of Curiosity," Psychological Bulletin: curiosity arises "when attention becomes focused on a gap in one's knowledge"; it is a deprivation state; curiosity is strongest when the gap is moderate and seems closeable (people are not curious about things they know nothing about), and it drives information-seeking. Critics note it underexplains interest without obvious gaps. — [PBS NOVA "How to stimulate curiosity"](https://www.pbs.org/wgbh/nova/article/how-to-stimulate-curiosity/); [Nautilus "Curiosity depends on what you already know"](https://nautil.us/curiosity-depends-on-what-you-already-know-235803); [eScholarship paper discussing information-gap theory](https://escholarship.org/content/qt9t43x6z1/qt9t43x6z1.pdf?t=sgibz4)
- **Calvert et al. (Child Development, 2020; Georgetown)**: 200+ children aged 3-6 learned the "add-1" rule with an intelligent Dora-based virtual character. **Stronger parasocial relationships and more math talk with the character predicted faster, more accurate math answers**; transfer to physical objects was better with an embodied character and **socially contingent replies**, mediated by math talk. The character was Wizard-of-Oz controlled (AI couldn't yet reliably parse young children's speech). Sample 63% European American, 96% college-educated families. — [Georgetown College news](https://college.georgetown.edu/news-story/study-finds-childrens-math-learning-improves-by-interacting-with-intelligent-characters/); [SRCD press release](https://www.srcd.org/news/study-young-children-can-learn-math-skills-intelligent-virtual-characters); [EdWeek coverage](https://edweek.org/leadership/digital-dora-the-explorer-helps-young-children-learn-math-study-finds/2019/12)
- **Kucirkova, Messer & Sheehy (2014)**, First Language: 18 preschoolers learned **more target words from personalized sections** (with their name) of a book than non-personalized sections. A later digital-book study found no word-learning advantage for personalized vs non-personalized digital books; personalization also increased children's self-referential talk. — [Open University ORO record](https://oro.open.ac.uk/40613); [Psychology Today (Kucirkova) "Personalized books: what you need to know"](https://www.psychologytoday.com/gb/blog/just-for-you/201809/personalized-books-for-children-what-you-need-to-know); [Lithub summary](https://lithub.com/remember-personalized-childrens-books-new-studies-show-they-might-have-scientific-benefits); [UCL Discovery (personalized digital book study)](https://discovery.ucl.ac.uk/1536711/)
- **Hirsh-Pasek, Zosh, Golinkoff et al. (2015)**, Psychological Science in the Public Interest 16(1):3-34: apps for 0-8 support learning when they foster **active (minds-on), engaged (not distracted by bells/whistles), meaningful (connected to child's life), and socially interactive** learning, *within a defined learning goal*; scaffolded exploration matters. — [APS page](https://www.psychologicalscience.org/index.php/publications/educational-apps.html); [New America summary](https://newamerica.org/education-policy/edcentral/new-framework-identify-truly-educational-apps); [ScienceDaily](https://www.sciencedaily.com/releases/2015/04/150420123021.htm)
- **Variable rewards / loot boxes:** Zendle & Cairns' survey of 7,000+ gamers found loot-box spending linked to problem gambling (link stronger than with alcohol/drug problems), replicated in adolescents; correlational, causation direction unknown. UK Gambling Commission estimated up to 31% of 11-16-year-olds had opened a loot box in the past year. Belgium (2018) classed paid loot boxes as gambling; House of Lords committee urged banning their sale to children. — [Zendle written evidence to UK Parliament](https://committees.parliament.uk/writtenevidence/83/html/); [Zendle & Cairns written evidence](https://committees.parliament.uk/writtenevidence/98535/html/); [IBA article](https://www.ibanet.org/article/B389FF33-433B-4ACB-BFC1-42CFCE22B60A); [bit-tech on Lords report](https://bit-tech.net/news/gaming/lords-call-for-urgent-regulation-of-loot-boxes-in-uk/1/)
- Radesky et al. (2022) list "attractive lures" such as stickers/trophies used to entice repeated engagement among manipulative designs in preschool apps (see §6). — [Michigan Medicine](https://www.michiganmedicine.org/health-lab/design-tricks-commonly-used-monetize-young-childrens-app-use)

### Inferences
- **Curiosity loop (safe):** pose a question the child can almost answer ("What's behind the door? It starts with the sound /m/…"), then resolve it. Reveal-on-completion of a *learning* act, not a random reveal. Keep gaps closable in one interaction for 2-5yo.
- **Flow for kids:** keep challenge just above current skill (~70-85% success is a common ed-tech heuristic, *not sourced here*), short loops (2.5yo: 30-90s activities; 5.5yo: 2-4 min; 8.5yo: 5-10 min) **[consensus, unsourced here]**.
- **Novelty & surprise:** fine as *content* variety (new animal, new story twist); avoid surprise as *reward randomisation*. Surprise rewards are SDT-safe only if not predictable/expected (once kids learn "surprise" stickers come every Nth task, they become expected).
- **Collection drive:** allow collections that are **earned deterministically by learning milestones** and that can be *completed* (finite sets), with no duplicates/rarity tiers, no purchasable packs, no "missing one!" pressure.
- **Narrative transportation:** a story spine (helping a character rebuild a village, find lost letters) supplies meaning (Hirsh-Pasek's "meaningful" pillar) and natural chapter endings that double as stopping cues.
- **Character:** one consistent, warm companion who talks *about the content* (Calvert: math talk mediated learning) and responds contingently to the child; the character must never guilt the child into continuing (that is "parasocial relationship pressure" — see §6). LLM-based voice characters could approximate the contingency Calvert's human operator provided, but raise privacy (voice data from children) and safety issues.
- **Personalization:** use the child's name and chosen avatar in stories; family members' names as story characters; benefits are modest and stronger in print/shared reading than digital; don't overdo self-focus.

### Gaps
- No child-specific (2-10) peer-reviewed study on flow states in apps was found in this session (Csikszentmihalyi's work is mostly adolescent/adult).
- No source found on narrative transportation effects specifically in under-10 learning games.
- Kucirkova's digital-book null result details (sample, measures) not fully retrieved.

## 4. Developmental stages and what 2.5yo / 5.5yo / 8.5yo can handle in an app

### Takeaway
The three children need three different interaction grammars: the 2.5yo needs tap-only, giant targets, audio-only, cause-and-effect; the 5.5yo can drag and follow 2-step spoken instructions but is a pre/emergent reader; the 8.5yo is a concrete-operational reader who can handle text, rules, strategy, and longer goals.

### Cited Findings
- **Gesture capability:** Abdul Aziz et al. (children 2-4): 4-year-olds performed all gestures tested; **2-year-olds performed tap and drag properly, learned flick quickly, but were weaker at drag-and-drop and pinch**; 3-year-olds struggled mainly with spread. Nacher et al. (2015, IJHCS) found 2-3-year-olds could do one-finger rotation and two-finger scale nearly as well as basic gestures — **sources conflict**. Vatavu et al. (3-6yo): good performance with tap, double tap and single-hand drag-and-drop; performance grows with age. — [Nacher et al. IJHCS author version (UPV)](https://riunet.upv.es/bitstream/10251/64752/3/VersionAutor.pdf); [Nacher short paper (UPV)](https://riunet.upv.es/bitstream/10251/77963/4/shortpaperVersionAutor.pdf)
- Practitioner guidance (UXPA, not peer-reviewed): children under 5 often lose contact mid-drag; suggests "tap-and-tap" (tap object, tap destination) instead of drag-and-drop. — [UXPA Magazine "Playful Interfaces"](https://oldmagazine.uxpa.org/?p=11776)
- 18-42-month-olds' common touchscreen errors: unintended movement, pressing too hard, imprecision partly due to small targets; children struggle more than adults with target size/location (CHI 2016). — [Woodward et al. CHI 2016](https://init.cise.ufl.edu/wp-content/uploads/sites/378/2017/05/Woodward-et-al-CHI2016_0.pdf); [unpaywall record of 2018 toddler study](https://unpaywall.org/10.1145%2F3242671.3242693)
- Calvert's work confirms 3-6-year-olds can learn early math (add-1) from a responsive character via spoken interaction. — [SRCD](https://www.srcd.org/news/study-young-children-can-learn-math-skills-intelligent-virtual-characters)

### Inferences
- **[consensus, unsourced here]** Piaget: 2-7 preoperational (symbolic play, egocentrism, no conservation, magical thinking — characters feel "real"); ~7-11 concrete operational (logic on concrete things, rules, classification, seriation, conservation). Theory of mind (false-belief) typically emerges ~4-5; executive functions (inhibition, working memory, flexibility) develop steeply 3-7 and continue through childhood. Toddlers cannot reliably distinguish ads/persuasive intent from content; understanding of persuasive intent typically develops ~7-8+.
- **Per-child interaction spec:**

| | 2.5yo toddler | 5.5yo boy | 8.5yo girl |
|---|---|---|---|
| Input | Tap only (anywhere-on-object counts); maybe simple drag with snap; no multi-touch, no long-press, no drag-and-drop to precise targets | Tap, drag with generous snapping, simple trace with wide tolerance; "tap-and-tap" fallback | Full gestures, drag-and-drop, tracing letters (Hebrew script/print), typing short words |
| Targets | Very large (aim ≥ ~2-3 cm physical, far apart; edges have no actions); forgiving hit areas | Large (≥ ~1.5-2 cm) | Standard child sizes (≥ ~1 cm / 48dp+) |
| Instructions | Audio + modelled demo (animated hand); zero text; one step | Audio, 1-2 steps, icons; letters only as learning content; replay-instruction button | Short Hebrew text + optional audio; multi-step rules OK |
| Content | Cause-effect, naming, colors, animals, sounds, simple sorting, songs | Phonological awareness, Hebrew letters & sounds (אותיות), counting to 20, shapes, patterns, early addition | Reading fluency/comprehension, math facts, multiplication, logic puzzles, English as L2, creative making |
| Session | ~3-5 min, ideally with adult (see §6) | ~10-15 min | ~15-25 min |
| Failure handling | No fail state; errors redirect | Gentle retry, hints after 2 misses | Visible challenge, optional hard mode, explicit progress |
| Navigation | No menus; parent selects; no exits to store/web | 2-3 big picture choices | Map/menu with choices, goals |
  (Physical target sizes and session lengths here are design heuristics, **not values from a cited study**.)
- Hebrew specifics: RTL layout; audio voice-over in natural Israeli Hebrew (and possibly Arabic/English/Russian toggles); niqqud for early readers; letter-sound learning matches Israeli kindergarten (גן חובה) curriculum — should be checked against Israeli Ministry of Education materials.

### Gaps
- No peer-reviewed millimetre-level touch-target recommendation for ages 2-3 was found (search result explicitly noted none).
- Developmental-milestone claims (Piaget, ToM, EF, persuasive-intent understanding) were not sourced in this session; the report writer should cite standard reviews (e.g., Wellman et al. 2001 ToM meta-analysis; Diamond 2013 EF review) if needed.

## 5. Sibling dynamics: avoiding comparison harm, cooperative play, older sibling as tutor

### Takeaway
Perceived differential treatment between siblings is linked to lower self-worth in the less-favoured child, with perceived fairness as the key moderator; therefore the app must never rank siblings against each other, and should instead make the 8.5yo a "teacher/helper" (protégé effect) and give each child a different, age-appropriate role in shared goals.

### Cited Findings
- Favoured siblings make downward comparisons and show better self-concept/well-being; youth receiving more favourable parental treatment than siblings show better adjustment (higher self-worth, less risk-taking). **Perceived fairness of differential treatment is a critical moderator**; absolute favoritism (not relative scores) related to self-esteem and rejection sensitivity in a 169-sibling study. — [PMC review (PMC5685545)](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5685545); [PMC5110249](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5110249)
- Three-wave study of 384 adolescent sibling dyads: reciprocal links between differential parenting, perceived partiality and self-worth; birth order related to self-worth and perceived differential treatment. — [PubMed 16402879](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/16402879/)
- Korean study (438 middle-schoolers): academic achievement *compared with a sibling* related to academic self-concept, mediated by maternal differential treatment. — [JKDP article](https://www.accesson.kr/jkdp/v.31/1/91/18365)
- **Protégé effect:** Chase, Chin, Oppezzo & Schwartz (2009), J. Science Education & Technology 18(4):334-352 — students (8th and 5th graders) put more effort into learning **for a teachable agent** than for themselves, spent more time reading, learned more; **effects strongest for lower-achieving children**; children attributed mental states and responsibility to their agent. — [Springer DOI](https://link.springer.com/doi/10.1007/s10956-009-9180-4); [Stanford PURL](https://purl.stanford.edu/zm369yx3532)

### Inferences
- **Hard rules:** no sibling leaderboards; no side-by-side scores or "your brother got 5 stars"; per-child progress is private to that child (+parent); parent dashboard shows each child against their *own* past, never against each other; age-normed comparisons never visible to kids.
- **Asymmetric cooperative modes:** shared family quest where each child contributes at their level (toddler taps animals to "wake them," 5.5yo finds the first letter, 8.5yo reads the clue) — everyone's piece is necessary; shared reward is visible to all.
- **Older sibling as tutor:** "Teach-the-character" mode for the 8.5yo (protégé effect), and a "helper" role where she can create a puzzle/quiz for her brother; frame her role as status ("guide/מדריכה"), but avoid making her the permanent babysitter or giving her power to judge him.
- **Fairness visibility:** equal access to features/time across kids; device-switching flows that respect each child's profile; reward types differ by age but are visibly "equally special."
- Gender: given Gunderson's finding that boys got more process praise, script parity checks for the girl and boy profiles.

### Gaps
- No study found on sibling co-play specifically on tablets/learning apps; the sibling literature found is mostly about parental differential treatment in adolescence.
- Protégé-effect studies are with 5th-8th graders; evidence for 8-year-olds teaching 5-year-olds via an app was not found (sibling-tutoring literature, e.g., Azmitia & Hesser, not searched).

## 6. Ethics & health: screen-time guidance, co-viewing, dark patterns, UK Children's Code, COPPA, loot boxes, stopping cues, sleep

### Takeaway
Guidance converges on: minimal solo screen use under 2, about ≤1 h/day of high-quality, ideally co-used media for 2-5, and context/content-based limits for 6+ (AAP's 2026 policy shifts emphasis from hour caps to quality/context). Dark patterns are pervasive in preschool apps (Radesky 2022: only 20% had none), and both UK and US regulation now target engagement-maximising and data-hungry design.

### Cited Findings
- **WHO (2019) guidelines for under-5s:** sedentary screen time **not recommended for under-1s and 1-year-olds**; for **2-4-year-olds, no more than 1 hour; less is better**; don't restrain children >1 hour at a time; 3-4-year-olds need ≥180 min physical activity/day incl. ≥60 min moderate-to-vigorous; 3-4-year-olds 10-13 h good-quality sleep (1-2yo: 11-14 h) with regular sleep/wake times; replace sedentary screen time with active play and caregiver interaction (reading, storytelling, singing, puzzles). — [WHO news release 24 Apr 2019](https://www.who.int/news/item/24-04-2019-to-grow-up-healthy-children-need-to-sit-less-and-play-more); [PAHO](https://www.paho.org/en/news/24-4-2019-grow-healthy-children-need-sit-less-and-play-more)

### Inferences
- **Health limits for this family (defaults, parent-adjustable):** toddler ≤ ~10-15 min per session, ideally co-played, well under WHO's 1 h/day *total* screen budget; 5.5yo ≤ ~15-20 min/session; 8.5yo ≤ ~20-30 min/session; no use within ~1 h of bedtime (bedtime lock based on parent-set schedule) **[AAP pre-bed guidance not re-sourced this session]**; app should *compete with nothing* — it should make "go play outside / read with Ima/Abba" an explicit endpoint.
- **Designed-to-end sessions:** sessions composed of a finite "episode" (e.g., 3 activities + a wind-down song/story), after which the character says goodbye and suggests an offline activity ("Let's go find 3 red things in your house!"). No autoplay to next episode; resume requires a deliberate action; for toddler profile, end-screen requires parent unlock to continue.
- **Character exit lines must be positive, not guilt-based:** "Great playing today! I'm going to sleep now — see you tomorrow!" — never "Don't leave me!" / "I'll be sad" (parasocial pressure per Radesky).
- **Zero dark patterns checklist (from Radesky taxonomy):** no fabricated timers; no lures/pop-ups for more play; no ads at all; no IAP visible to children (purchases only behind parent gate); no navigation traps; no "your streak will die" alarms; no push notifications to child; no randomized paid rewards.
- **Privacy:** collect minimal data; no third-party ad SDKs; on-device storage where possible; voice/LLM features off by default and parent-consented; comply with COPPA (if any US users), UK AADC (if UK users), GDPR-K, and Israel's Privacy Protection Law (Amendment 13, in force 2025 — **not researched here**).
- **JME design:** "play together" prompts for parent (2-3 conversation starters per episode, in Hebrew), parent can play the second role; toddler mode should ideally *require* lap-play.

## 7. Concrete product rules: reward system, praise scripts, safe streaks, stopping mechanics

### Takeaway
Make mastery, story progress, and creation the core rewards; give tangible-like rewards only as unexpected, informational acknowledgements of learning milestones; replace individual loss-aversion streaks with forgiving, family-level "habit gardens"; and build every session to end gracefully.

### Cited Findings
- Expected tangible rewards undermine preschoolers' interest; unexpected ones don't (Lepper et al. 1973). — [Psychology Notes HQ](https://www.psychologynoteshq.com/overjustification-effect/)
- Tangible rewards substantially undermine intrinsic motivation (Deci et al. 1999), with Cameron & Pierce disputing generality. — [Deci et al. 1999 PDF](https://home.ubalt.edu/ntygmitc/642/Articles%20syllabus/Deci%20Koestner%20Ryan%20meta%20IM%20psy%20bull%2099.pdf); [PMC review](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC2731358/)
- Process praise > person praise; avoid inflated praise. — [Gunderson 2013 PMC](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3655123); [Cimpian 2007 (BPS)](https://bps.org.uk/research-digest/how-praise-children); [Brummelman 2014 (OSU)](https://artsandsciences.osu.edu/news/high-praise-could-backfire-kids-low-self-esteem)
- Lures (stickers/trophies) and parasocial pressure are documented manipulative patterns. — [Michigan Medicine on Radesky 2022](https://www.michiganmedicine.org/health-lab/design-tricks-commonly-used-monetize-young-childrens-app-use)
- Nudge toward breaks; offer pause-and-save. — [ICO Children's Code PDF](https://ico.org.uk/media/for-organisations/uk-gdpr-guidance-and-resources/childrens-information/childrens-code-guidance-and-resources/age-appropriate-design-a-code-of-practice-for-online-services-2-1.pdf)
- Learning-by-teaching increases effort, especially for lower achievers. — [Chase et al. 2009](https://link.springer.com/doi/10.1007/s10956-009-9180-4)

### Inferences (product rules)
**A. Reward system that preserves intrinsic motivation**
1. **Core loop reward = the activity's own outcome** (the bridge gets built, the story continues, the creature you taught learns) — not points.
2. **No "do X to get Y" announcements.** Never pre-announce prizes for tasks ("finish 5 to win a sticker").
3. **Surprise & delight, irregular but non-random-for-value:** occasional unexpected celebrations tied to a *real* learning moment (first time reading a 3-letter word); no rarity tiers, no gacha, no duplicates.
4. **Informational badges only:** a badge names the skill ("You can now count to 20!"), acting as competence feedback; show a "skills tree/garden" growing rather than a coin balance.
5. **No currency** (or, if any, non-purchasable, non-gambled, spent on creative self-expression like decorating a room — and never shown in comparison to siblings).
6. **Creation as reward:** unlock tools/materials for drawing, building, song-making — tools the child uses autonomously.
7. **Phase out reward density with age/mastery:** more celebration for toddler (sensory feedback is the reward), sparser and more informational for 8.5yo.

**B. Praise scripts (system voice)**
- Allowed: describe action ("You matched all the shapes"), strategy ("You sounded it out"), effort/persistence ("You kept going on a tricky one"), improvement ("Faster than last time"), choice ("You picked the hard level — brave").
- Banned: trait labels ("smart," "genius," "good reader" as identity), superlatives ("incredible," "perfect," "best"), comparison ("better than your brother"), conditional love from characters ("I like you when you win").
- Error scripts: never buzzers/red X for under-6; "Hmm, let's look again"; hint after 2 tries; auto-solve with modelling after 3; for 8.5yo, "Not yet — want a hint or another try?"
- Rotate ~10-20 script variants per category per age to avoid repetitiveness; maintain gender parity of process praise.

**C. Safe streaks / habit design**
- Prefer a **family "garden" or "village"** that grows with any family member's learning days — shared, non-comparative, cooperative.
- **Weekly target, not daily** (e.g., "3 adventure days this week"); missing a day does nothing bad.
- **Automatic streak freezes** (Shabbat/holiday/weekend rest days built in — culturally fitting for Israeli families; sick days; parent-declared rest days).
- **No loss framing:** never show "streak lost"; growth pauses visually (plant "rests") rather than dying; no notifications to children.
- Toddler: no streaks at all.
- Celebrate *returning* rather than *consecutive* days.

**D. Stopping mechanics ("designed to end")**
- Session = finite episode with visible progress ("3 stops on today's map"), then a wind-down ritual (song/story/goodbye) and an offline "mission."
- Parent-set daily budget per child; soft warning ("one more activity then our friend goes to sleep"), then graceful close with auto-save; no mid-activity cut-offs.
- Bedtime lock; night mode that discourages use; no autoplay; no "just one more" prompts.
- Re-entry after end requires a parent gate (toddler & 5.5yo).
- Parent dashboard: per-child learning summary + suggested offline follow-ups + conversation starters (JME).

**E. Sibling rules**
- Separate profiles, private progress; cooperative family quests with role asymmetry; "teacher mode" for the 8.5yo; no leaderboards or cross-child stats visible to children.

**F. Ethics guardrails**
- No ads, no third-party trackers, no child-facing purchases, no loot boxes, no fabricated urgency, no guilt from characters, no social features with strangers, data minimisation and high-privacy defaults (COPPA 2025 amendments, UK AADC, GDPR, Israeli privacy law).
- Run a Radesky-taxonomy dark-pattern audit before each release.

### Gaps
- No empirical study was found testing "family streaks" or "streak freezes" with young children; streak recommendations above are inferences from SDT, loss-aversion concerns, and AADC wellbeing-nudge guidance.
- No direct evidence on optimal reward density by age for app-based learning in 2-10-year-olds.
